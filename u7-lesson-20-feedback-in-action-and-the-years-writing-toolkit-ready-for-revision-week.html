1 · Whole-class feedback · 2¼ minutes · the most common next steps · before → after

Next steps 1–3: the introduction, the thesis, the body paragraphs

Every Before is an invented sentence, written for this lesson, on a different question: To what extent was Arsinoe II a powerful queen?

1Set the contextwho, when, where, in expanded noun groups; limit the key term

BeforeArsinoe II was a queen of Egypt. She was married to Ptolemy II. People say she was powerful.

AfterArsinoe II, a Macedonian Greek queen who married three kings, the last her brother Ptolemy II, died in 270 or 268 BCE; here, power means a say in what the king and his court decided, not only the honours paid to her.

Why it works: who, when and what in expanded noun groups (a Macedonian Greek queen who married three kings), then one limit on the key word, power, before the thesis.

2Thesis: answer with a degreea judgement of degree that previews the reasons in order; no sentence announcing the essay

BeforeThis essay will discuss whether Arsinoe II was a powerful queen, which is a debated question.

AfterArsinoe II was powerful only to a limited extent in what the evidence can show: the coin and the cult honour her, but mostly after her death, and the decree that names her may show courtesy to her, not her influence on policy.

Why it works: a degree from the scale (only to a limited extent), three reasons previewed in the order the paragraphs will make them, and no announcement.

3Body paragraphs: judge, don’t narratea topic sentence linked to the thesis, then the five moves, ending in the thesis’s degree

BeforeArsinoe married three kings. The last was her brother. Then she died. Then she was worshipped.

AfterHer honours came mostly after her death, which is the first reason her power can be shown only to a limited extent. A loan of 221–220 BCE is dated by ‘the kanephoros of Arsinoe Philadelphus’ (Select Papyri I 65), which suggests that her cult still had its own officer about fifty years after she died. … On balance, the cult shows how long she was honoured, not what she decided in life.

Why it works: it opens with a judgement linked to the thesis, says what the evidence suggests, not what happened next, and, after the Evaluate (…), resolves in the thesis’s degree.

1 · Whole-class feedback · 2¼ minutes · the most common next steps · before → after

Next steps 4–6: evidence, language, the conclusion

Every Before is an invented sentence, written for this lesson, on the same different question: Arsinoe II’s power.

4Evidence: attribute every voicewho says it, and through whom you know it

BeforeSome historians say that Arsinoe II was a powerful queen.

AfterStefano Caneva (2013) reports that a picture of Arsinoe II as a very powerful court schemer dominated much twentieth-century writing, and lists Tarn (1913), Macurdy (1932) and Pomeroy (1984) among its supporters.

Why it works: it names the reporter and the year, gives the view to the historians who held it, and shows how you know it: through Caneva, not from their own pages.

5Language: match the modalityas sure as the evidence allows; link each sentence to the last

BeforeThe Athenian decree proves that Arsinoe controlled Ptolemy II’s policy.

AfterThe Athenian decree has Ptolemy act ‘in accordance with the predilection (proairesei) both of his ancestors and of his sister’, which may point to her influence on policy; yet Attic Inscriptions Online notes that ‘we do not know if this mention of her is posthumous’, so the clause is weaker evidence than it first appears.

Why it works: proves and controlled claim more than one clause can carry; may point to fits the evidence, and yet … so links the doubt to the claim.

6Conclude with a refined judgementthe thesis in the same degree, the reasons drawn together, a decisive end

BeforeIn conclusion, this essay has looked at a coin, a cult and a decree, and Arsinoe II was important in all of them.

AfterUltimately, Arsinoe II was powerful only to a limited extent: the coin and the cult show how long she was honoured, not what she decided, and the decree that names her may show courtesy, not policy.

Why it works: it keeps the thesis’s degree, draws the three reasons together, adds no new evidence, and says what the essay found, not what it did.

3 · Toolkit review · part 3: match · models G–L

Match the cards to the models: G to L

G

Examine two views of how the Great Pyramid’s builders were treated.

In Godley’s translation, Herodotus reports his Egyptian informants’ claim that Khufu “compelled all the Egyptians to work for him” (Herodotus 2.124), whereas the excavators of Heit el-Ghurab present the builders as crews whom the crown housed and fed (Ancient Egypt Research Associates n.d.).

H

Describe the Met’s gold coin of Arsinoe II. (3 marks)

The coin is a gold octadrachm, 2.8 cm across, now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. One side shows Arsinoe II’s veiled head in profile; the other shows two horns of plenty and the Greek words for ‘of Arsinoe Philadelphos’. It was struck after her death, in about 261–246 BCE.

I

Outline the period this unit studies. (3 marks)

The unit begins with Classical Athens, from about 500 BCE to Alexander’s death in 323 BCE. Alexander took Egypt in 332/1 BCE, and after his death the Ptolemies, a Macedonian Greek dynasty, ruled it as a Greek kingdom. The period ends in 30 BCE with the death of Cleopatra VII, the last of the Ptolemies.

J

Explain why a citizen mother mattered more after 451/0 BCE.

After 451/0 BCE a citizen wife’s own birth mattered more, mainly because Pericles’s law confined citizenship “to persons of citizen birth on both sides” (Athenaion Politeia 26.4), which meant that her sons’ rights now depended on her descent; although bearing legitimate heirs had been a wife’s role before the law, the law mattered most because it made her birth, not only her marriage, decide her sons’ status.

K

Account for Apollodorus’s attitude to wives. (question written for this card; answer from Lesson 3)

Apollodorus, a citizen prosecuting the foreign woman Neaera before a jury of male citizens, presents wives as bearing “legitimate children” because his case set citizen marriage apart from life with a hetaira, but he spoke to win a trial, so his words show what he expected jurors to accept rather than how every marriage worked.

L

Propose why Euxitheus had to explain his mother’s work.

One possible explanation is that paid work was thought beneath a citizen woman, since, as Euxitheus tells it, his opponent used his mother’s work as a nurse against his citizenship; this cannot explain everything, however, because it cannot explain why her work should count against her birth when, as Euxitheus says, “many women” of “civic birth” had done such work when times were hard (Demosthenes 57.45).

If a papyrus recorded crews held without release, rations or pay, I would have to move back towards Herodotus’s picture of a people brought to “utter misery”; on present evidence, though, Herodotus’s three-month gangs, the crews’ food and their organised units suggest that the crown organised and fed them, probably as a duty owed to the king, so Herodotus is supported only in part.

O

Compare the two vases as pictures of women. (question written for this card; answer from Lesson 5)

The two vases are alike in showing femininity as adornment among women, but they differ in what the women are doing. Whereas the Art Institute describes nine women on the pyxis, presumably in the women’s quarters, with a mirror, a lyre and a book roll, the Royal Ontario Museum says its fragments show women preparing two brides, with Aphrodite and Paitho among the figures. Both present a woman’s world as adornment and the company of other women, through a mirror on the pyxis and jewellery offered to a bride on the fragments. Of these, the likeness matters more, because both are painters’ pictures of women among women, with the things of adornment, so they probably show an ideal of femininity, not the whole of women’s lives.

3 · Toolkit review · part 3: match · models P–Q

Match the cards to the models: P and Q

P

To what extent did the way a marriage was made change from Classical Athens to Ptolemaic Egypt?

The way a marriage was made changed largely in form, not in who decided. Whereas an Athenian bride was given by her kyrios through a spoken pledge (the engyē), Demetria was given “from her father Leptines, Coan, and her mother Philotis” in a written contract of 311/310 BCE that set out duties for both husband and wife; in both, however, the arrangements were between the bride’s family and the groom, so the change was largely one of form rather than of who decided.

Q

Roman writers on a woman in command (Dio and Tacitus): what does this language reveal about the Roman writers, rather than about Boudicca? (Unit 4, Lesson 12’s question, with its context added)

That Roman writers expected war and rule to belong to men. Dio treats defeat by a woman as a special shame, and praises her intelligence as unusual for a woman; Tacitus has to explain a woman leader to his readers as a British custom, and gives Suetonius a sneer at an army of women. So these words are strong evidence of how Romans judged a woman in command, and weak evidence of how Boudicca herself led.

Gender works as a weapon both ways: Tacitus’s Boudicca uses it to shame the men who will not fight, and in 12.40 even Britons are “stung with shame” at a woman’s rule. Every one of these lines was written, or composed, by a Roman senator.

3 · Toolkit review · part 3: match · models R–S

Match the cards to the models: R and S

R

How useful is Lysias 1 for understanding the lives of women in Classical Athens? (5 marks)

Written by the speechwriter Lysias for a husband on trial for killing his wife’s lover, Lysias 1 was spoken in his defence to a court of Athenian men. It is useful for how a husband described his wife’s role, since Euphiletus praises her as at first ‘a clever, frugal housekeeper’, and for the layout of a small house ‘with the women’s quarters above and the men’s below’. However, every detail serves his defence, persuading the jurors that he trusted his wife and set no trap, so the speech shows her as he needed them to see her. Lysias 1 is therefore reliable for how an Athenian husband wanted his household to appear to a jury in about 400 BCE, but not for his wife’s experience, because her words reach us only through his defence.

S

To what extent was the mistreatment of the Iceni the main reason for the revolt of 60 or 61 CE?

The mistreatment of the Iceni was only partly the main reason for the revolt, because the imperial cult, centred on the temple of the Divine Claudius, gave Britons beyond the Iceni a grievance of their own. As Tacitus reports, the temple was ‘a citadel, as it seemed, of perpetual tyranny’ (Annals 14.31), and the men chosen as its priests ‘had to squander their whole fortunes’ (14.31), which suggests that Roman rule could feel oppressive through religion and expense, not only through force, and that the colony’s neighbours had reasons to rise that owed nothing to Prasutagus’s family. Yet the words ‘as it seemed’ show that this is Tacitus’s view of how Britons felt, written by a Roman senator about 55 years later; and in his account it was the Iceni, angered by their own wrongs, who ‘stirred to revolt the Trinobantes and others’ (14.31). On balance, the temple helps to explain why the revolt spread beyond the Iceni, but not why it began, so the mistreatment of the Iceni was only partly its main reason.

Practice and review · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A Resolve should say the same degree as the Judge, in the question’s own words. True — it may say it more sharply, but it must not change it, and it links back to the question.
  2. 2A Resolve is a good place to add one last piece of evidence. False — it weighs the evidence the paragraph has already given; new evidence needs its own analysis.
  3. 3Assess, evaluate and “to what extent” all ask for a judgement, so all three can use Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve. True — they share a structure; the question’s words tell you whether to judge by degree (to what extent), by a criterion (evaluate) or by size or value (assess).
  4. 4“Outline” and “explain” should be answered at the same length whatever the marks. False — the marks set the length (department advice) and the verb sets the kind: outline gives the main points, briefly; explain gives causes and their effects.
  5. 5In an OVPRL paragraph the judgement of reliability is the same for every question. False — reliability is always for a question: “reliable for … but not for … because …”.
  6. 6A thesis should begin “This essay will …” so the reader knows the plan. False — the thesis is the preview: a degree and its reasons, in order, with no sentence announcing the essay.

Practice and review · 2 of 4 · writing · the Resolve

Fix each Resolve

Each paragraph is invented, written for this lesson, and ends in a weak Resolve. Rewrite the Resolve, then show one answer.

  1. Same degree. Question: To what extent does the Hegeso stele show how Athenian women lived? Judge: “The Hegeso stele shows how Athenian women lived only to a limited extent, because it probably presents an ideal a family chose.” Resolve: “So the stele shows exactly how Athenian women lived.” Ultimately, then, the stele shows how Athenian women lived only to a limited extent, because it probably presents an ideal that a family chose, not a record of daily life.
  2. Nothing new. Question: To what extent does P. Eleph. 1 show how Greek marriages were made in Egypt? Judge: “P. Eleph. 1 shows it only to a limited extent, because it is one contract.” Resolve: “Also, Select Papyri I 65 is a loan in which a woman acts with her guardian.” On balance, then, P. Eleph. 1 shows how Greek marriages were made in Egypt only to a limited extent, because it is one contract, not a sample of marriages.
  3. Say what you found. Question: To what extent does Herodas, Mime 1 show what Egypt was like? Judge: “It shows it only to a limited extent, because its list of Egypt’s attractions is a go-between’s sales talk.” Resolve: “In conclusion, this paragraph has looked at Herodas’s Mime 1.” Ultimately, then, Mime 1 shows what Egypt was like only to a limited extent: it is better evidence of what a poet could make a go-between promise than of Egypt itself.

Exercise 3 · rewrite your conclusion

Choose the historian whose view bears on your Lesson 19 response. Rewrite your conclusion so that it weighs that view. Then add one sentence: does the view change your judgement’s degree, or only what it rests on?

A strong rewrite includes
  • precise attribution in the present tense, through the right person: “Bagnall argues …”, “Henry, as Wallace quotes her, says …”, “Bartels reports that Carney …”;
  • what the view covers and what it does not: Bagnall’s words are about the documents he lists, Henry’s sentence is about Aspasia, and Carney’s view is Bartels’s account of it;
  • a weighed response, not agreement by default: what does your own evidence add, or leave out?
  • a judgement that is refined, not abandoned, and still ends the conclusion;
  • no new evidence beyond the historian’s view itself.

Practice and review · 4 of 4 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What does NESA say assess means, and what must a Resolve for an ‘assess’ answer do? ‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’ The Resolve keeps the Judge’s degree, answers the question’s words and adds no new evidence.
  2. Name the five moves of a body paragraph, in order. Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve.
  3. Name the five parts of OVPRL, in order, and say how reliability is judged. Origin, Value, Purpose, Reliability, Limitations; always for a question: “reliable for … but not for … because …”.
  4. What does a thesis do, and what must it never do? Answers with a degree and previews the main reasons, in order; never announces the essay (“This essay will …”).
  5. How do you work out how long a 5-mark answer should take? Minutes a mark = writing minutes ÷ total marks, then 5 × that rate (department advice). On our sample paper, 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5, so 7½ minutes.
  6. What makes a revision target strong? It names a dot point or a checklist number and one action you could do and tick.
