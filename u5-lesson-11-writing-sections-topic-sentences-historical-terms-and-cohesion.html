Unit 5 · Historical investigation · Lesson 11

Writing sections: topic sentences, historical terms and cohesion

How does a section state its claim, use its terms precisely and link to the section before it?

Year 11 Ancient History · Present with → or space · Read on a phone with R

Review · books closed · 6 minutes · press K for the timer

Five questions and a writing item

  1. Name the four parts of a report, in order. An introduction; sections, each with a heading that states a claim; a conclusion; and a reference list.
  2. Name a synthesis connective for two sources that differ. whereas · while …, … · unlike · in contrast · however (the “differ” row of the connective bank).
  3. NESA defines synthesise as ‘Putting together various elements to make a whole.’ What does a synthesis do in this course? It builds one claim from several sources, joined with connectives, and says what the evidence cannot show.
  4. What is corroboration, and what must be true of the two sources? Support for a claim from an independent source. The two sources must reach the claim from different evidence: a source that only repeats or reports the other is the same evidence again.
  5. In a citation, what does n.d. mean, and when do you use it? ‘No date.’ It stands in place of the year when a source shows none, as in (Ancient Egypt Research Associates n.d.).
  6. Writing · join these with a synthesis connective and cite both: ‘Herodotus mentions onions. Animal bones were found in the town.’ Herodotus reports that an inscription on the pyramid recorded money spent on the workmen’s radishes, onions and garlic (Herodotus 2.125); similarly, the Ministry reads the animal bones found in the town as a sign that the state kept its workers healthy (Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) n.d.). This model joins what the two agree on (the crews were fed), so similarly. Whereas is right too if you contrast what they show (vegetables against meat), as Lesson 8’s synthesis did. Either way, cite both sources.

Key vocabulary · add to the unit word wall · the term bank

Words for sections, and terms to use precisely

Writing termsMeaning
Claim headingA section heading that states the section’s answer.
Topic sentenceThe first sentence of a section; it answers the sub-question and links to the thesis.
Summary nounA noun that sums up the last idea and carries it forward: ‘This provisioning…’
Given → newBegin a sentence with what the reader already knows (the given) and end it with the new idea. The new idea then becomes the given of the next sentence.
Research termsMeaning
Primary / secondary sourceA source from the time studied (an inscription, a papyrus, an object) / a later work about it (a historian’s book, an excavation report). Which one it is depends on your question.
Research termsMeaning
ProvenanceWhere a piece of evidence comes from: who wrote or found it, and when.
CorroborationSupport for a claim from an independent source.
Interpretation / representationA historian’s evidence-based explanation of the past / a depiction of the past shaped by its maker’s time and purpose.
SignificanceThe importance of something, judged for a particular purpose.
CausationThe causes of an event or development, and what followed from them.
Continuity / changeWhat stays the same over time / what becomes different.

1 · Claims, links and terms · I do — the class model, section 2 (1 of 3)

A claim heading, then a topic sentence that carries the hedge

A revision move you can copy. Last lesson this section’s heading read ‘The builders’ town points to crews that the crown organised and fed’. Both are claim headings. Cutting a heading to its core claim is allowed when the topic sentence carries the hedge and the limit.

The class question (closed): How far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?

2 · Fed at the crown’s expense

Evidence from the sites begins with the crown’s provisioning of its crews, the clearest evidence in this report that the “utter misery” in Herodotus’s account (Herodotus 2.124) may overstate their hardship, although the builders’ town may belong mainly to later kings. Ancient Egypt Research Associates (AERA) (n.d.), the team that has excavated the town, describe a settlement that the crown created for the work, with bakeries, kitchens and royal grain silos. The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) (n.d.) reads the large quantity of animal bones from the town as a sign that the state kept its workers healthy, which fits Herodotus’s report that an inscription recorded what was spent on the workmen’s radishes, onions and garlic (Herodotus 2.125). The Ministry also reports that most of the people buried in the town’s cemetery show signs of heavy physical work and many had broken bones, though most of these had healed well, so the work was hard even if injuries were treated (Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) n.d.). The town’s date is a limit: AERA links it to Menkaure, Khafre and probably Khufu (Ancient Egypt Research Associates n.d.), but the Ministry places its workers in the reigns of Khafre and Menkaure, with an older settlement beneath that “may date to Khufu’s reign” (Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) n.d.).

Claim heading: short and sharp. It says who bore the cost, and a source could support or challenge it

Topic sentence: it opens from section 1’s last idea (evidence from the sites), answers the sub-question and links to the thesis. It carries the hedge (may overstate), a limit on its own strength (in this report) and the limit on the evidence (although …)

Evidence sentences: last lesson’s, unchanged: each source cited where it is used, ending on a limit

Which words in the topic sentence limit its claim?

Three limits. In this report limits the clearest evidence; may overstate hedges the claim about hardship; although the builders’ town may belong mainly to later kings names what the evidence cannot show.

3 · The crews worked in organised groups, probably as a duty owed to the king

This limit on the town’s date gives Merer’s papyri more weight for Khufu’s reign: they suggest that the crews worked in organised groups, probably as a duty owed to the king. Papyri found at Wadi al-Jarf, from late in Khufu’s reign, include accounts of regular food deliveries and the daily logbook of the inspector Merer, which records his men carrying limestone from the Tura quarries to Giza (Tallet and Marouard 2014). Weiss (2022), reporting Tallet’s reading, describes Merer’s unit, or phyle, as about 40 men, one of four in a larger team, so the crews seem to have worked in organised groups. Herodotus’s gangs that served for three months at a time (Herodotus 2.124) fit service in turns, and Lehner judges that “it seems that everybody owed a labor tax”, although “it may not have been entirely coercion” (Lehner 1997). The service was therefore probably a duty owed to the king, but not necessarily a harsh one: Weiss (2022) notes that it is hard to say whether the quarry workers and stone-lifters were treated as well as Merer’s boatmen.

Claim heading: unchanged from last lesson. The thesis’s own hedge, probably, is carried into it

The link back: This + a summary noun. Limit sums up the last idea of section 2 (the town’s date) in one word

Given → new: the given is the date limit; the new idea is the papyri, which are dated to Khufu’s reign

The claim, linked to the thesis: it repeats the thesis’s words, with one hedge for each claim (suggest, probably)

What would be wrong with opening section 3 with This provisioning?

It points past the sentence just before. Section 2 ends on the town’s date, not on food. A summary noun must sum up the idea the reader has just read, or the link is unclear.

1 · Claims, links and terms · I do — a historical term misused: spot it, then fix it · then cold call

Fix the term

Each sentence is an invented example, written for this lesson, about the class model. Use the term bank in your key vocabulary list.

The sentence · one term is misusedThe fix
‘The web page of Ancient Egypt Research Associates is a primary source for the builders’ town.’Secondary source. It is a modern work by the excavators about the town. A primary source is from the time studied, such as the papyri found at Wadi al-Jarf. Which one it is depends on your question.
‘Weiss (2022) corroborates Ancient Egypt Research Associates on the bakeries.’Not corroboration. Weiss reports the dig directed by Lehner, so it is the same evidence again: the two are dependent. Corroboration needs an independent source.
‘The provenance of Merer’s papyri is that they are very reliable.’Provenance is where the evidence comes from: the papyri were found at Wadi al-Jarf, and the logbook was kept by the inspector Merer late in Khufu’s reign. How reliable it is comes later, after the provenance, and for a question.
‘Lehner’s suggestion that everybody owed a labour tax is a representation.’Interpretation. It is a historian’s evidence-based explanation. A representation is a depiction of the past shaped by its maker’s time and purpose, such as a painting.

What do the four fixes have in common?

Each term has one exact meaning. Using it for something else makes the claim wrong, even when the rest of the sentence is right. Check the term against its definition before you write it.

Which heading best signals the claim of section 2?

Section 2 answers the sub-question What does the builders’ town show? One heading states a claim. The others only name a topic.

C: only C states a claim

A source could support or challenge C. A names things; B and D name a question (how? what?) without answering it. None of them says anything a source could challenge. A claim heading answers its sub-question.

Sub-question: Why did the Babylonians and Medes fight together, and how did they take Nineveh?

We do · in pairs · 3 minutes

What does the narrating version leave out?

It tells what happened but makes no claim. It does not answer why together, it does not link to the thesis, and it names no limit.

A model repair · written for this lesson

The alliance of the Babylonians and Medes was the immediate cause of Nineveh’s fall: both at war with Assyria, they joined forces and besieged the city for three months in 612 BCE (Livius.org 2020), although the chronicle cannot show how far drought had already weakened Assyria.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · homework or revision · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A heading such as ‘The papyri’ is a claim heading. False — it names a topic. A claim heading answers the sub-question, so a source could support or challenge it.
  2. 2The topic sentence is the first sentence of a section; it answers the sub-question and links to the thesis. True — it tells the reader what the section will prove and how it serves the thesis.
  3. 3A claim heading may be shorter than the claim, as long as the topic sentence carries the hedge and the limit. True — the hedge and the limit are not lost; they move into the sentence that follows.
  4. 4You can open a section with ‘This limit …’ even if the sentence before is about something else. False — a summary noun must sum up the sentence just before it, or the reader cannot tell what this points to.
  5. 5A modern web page about an excavation is a primary source for the ancient town. False — it is a later work about the town, so it is a secondary source for that question. A primary source is from the time studied, such as a papyrus found there.
  6. 6If two sources agree, they corroborate each other. False — only if they are independent. A source that repeats or reports the other is the same evidence again.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · homework or revision · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. Explain what makes a heading a claim heading. It answers the sub-question, so a source could support or challenge it; a topic label only names a subject. It may be short if the topic sentence carries the hedge and the limit.
  2. Describe what a topic sentence does. It is the first sentence of a section. It answers the sub-question, links to the thesis, and carries the hedge and the limit.
  3. Explain two ways to link a section to the one before. Open with this + a summary noun that sums up the last idea (This limit …), or start from the last idea and add the new one (given → new).
  4. Why can a summary noun point to nothing? If it does not sum up the sentence just before, the reader cannot tell what this refers to.
  5. Name three historical terms and give a one-line meaning of each. For example: provenance, where evidence comes from (who wrote or found it, and when); corroboration, support for a claim from an independent source; secondary source, a later work about the time studied.
  6. What does given → new mean? Begin a sentence with what the reader already knows and end it with the new idea, which becomes the start of the next sentence.
