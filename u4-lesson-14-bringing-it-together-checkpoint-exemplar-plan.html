Connect · 1 minute · the Lesson 13 review item the class found hardest

First, one thing to fix from the last review

1 · Suetonius’s ground

Describe the ground where, Tacitus says, Suetonius chose to fight the final battle.

A position reached by a narrow defile, ‘closed in at the rear by a forest’, with an open plain in front (Annals 14.34). The site is unknown: probably near Watling Street; Mancetter is one suggestion. Tacitus says Suetonius chose it; Dio says he was ‘compelled, contrary to his judgment’, to fight (62.8).

2 · Classicianus

Who was Julius Classicianus, and what did he tell Rome?

The new procurator, ‘sent as successor to Catus’ (Annals 14.38). At odds with Suetonius, he wrote that ‘no cessation of fighting must be expected, unless Suetonius were superseded’ (14.38). His tombstone, found in London, names his post (RIB 12).

3 · Turpilianus

Who was Petronius Turpilianus, and how does Tacitus judge him?

The governor who replaced Suetonius (Annals 14.39). Tacitus gives two verdicts: ‘tame inaction under the honourable name of peace’ (Annals 14.39), and sent ‘to initiate a milder rule’ (Agricola 16).

4 · Writing: name the agent

Rewrite “Londinium was abandoned.” so that it names who acted.

Suetonius Paulinus abandoned Londinium, choosing, in Tacitus’s words, ‘to save the province at the cost of a single town’ (Annals 14.33). The active voice is right here: the decision was his.

5 · Prasutagus’s will

What did Prasutagus’s will do, and what happened after his death?

He made the emperor his heir along with his two daughters, hoping to keep his kingdom safe. ‘But the reverse was the result’: his kingdom was plundered by centurions and his family mistreated (Annals 14.31).

6 · The writers’ distance

About how long after the revolt did Tacitus and Dio write? Why does the gap matter?

Tacitus about 55 years later; Dio about 150 years later, in the early 3rd century CE. Neither saw the revolt: both relied on earlier accounts, and each wrote for readers of his own time.

1dot point 1 where the Iceni livedA Roman name and the coins: Tacitus names the Iceni (Annals 12.31, on 47 CE) but does not say where they lived; finds of Iceni coins cluster in Norfolk, north Suffolk and fenland Cambridgeshire, so historians match the name to the place. Whether the coin legend ECEN is the people’s name or a person’s is debated.
2dot point 2 client king; ‘Romanisation’A client king was a local ruler allowed to keep his kingdom as Rome’s ally, in return for loyalty (Prasutagus of the Iceni; Cogidubnus; Cartimandua). ‘Romanisation’ is a modern term for the spread of Roman ways of life, and historians disagree about it: Millett (1990) stressed British elites adopting Roman ways; critics such as Mattingly (2006) stress how differently Britons experienced Roman rule.
3dot point 3 the two officialsThe governor, Suetonius Paulinus: the emperor’s army commander and chief judge in the province. The procurator, Decianus Catus: he ran the province’s finances and taxes for the emperor. Catus was never the governor.
4dot point 4 Colchester’s burnt layerShows: a great fire across almost the whole town, dated by the coins and pottery in and under it (if the layers are undisturbed) to a time consistent with 60 or 61 CE. Cannot show: who lit it, why, or how many died (human remains are very rarely found); the link to the revolt comes from matching the date to Tacitus.
5dot point 5 the dead in the townsTacitus: “About seventy thousand” citizens and allies in the places he names (Annals 14.33). Dio: ‘eighty thousand of the Romans and of their allies perished’ (62.1). Claims: round numbers, written about 55 and about 150 years later, and neither writer says how the dead were counted.

2 · An annotated exemplar · I do — read it, then think aloud · 1 of 5: the introduction

A strong response, move by move: the introduction

Assess the consequences of the revolt for the Celts and the Romans. A different question from yours: watch the moves, not the content.

A teacher’s exemplar · written for this lesson · the introduction

In 60 or 61 CE the Iceni of eastern Britain and their Trinovantian neighbours rose against Rome and destroyed Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium, before the governor, Suetonius Paulinus, defeated them in a battle whose site is still unknown. Here, consequences means what the revolt led to for each side, both in the months after the battle and in how Britain was ruled for years afterwards. The revolt’s consequences were far heavier for the Celts than for the Romans, because a punitive campaign and famine fell on the Britons, while Rome replaced a governor rather than losing a province; its longer-term consequences, a milder rule and new walls at Camulodunum, touched both sides, but they are harder to link to the revolt with certainty.

What it does

Context: who, when and where, packed into expanded noun groups (the governor, Suetonius Paulinus; a battle whose site is still unknown)

Key term limited: what consequences will cover: both sides, short and long term

Thesis: a judgement of degree on the question’s own words (far heavier … than)

Preview: the three arguments, in the order the paragraphs make them, with a concession (but … harder to link); no sentence announcing the essay

2 · An annotated exemplar · I do · 2 of 5: body paragraph 1 · our Lesson 7 checklist, move by move

Body paragraph 1: the five moves

Assess the consequences of the revolt for the Celts and the Romans. Paragraph 1 argues the first reason in the preview.

A teacher’s exemplar · written for this lesson · body paragraph 1 · the Celts

For the Celts, the most severe consequences came in the months after the battle, when Rome punished the peoples who had rebelled. Tacitus reports that ‘whatever tribes still wavered or were hostile were ravaged with fire and sword’, and that famine followed, because ‘they had been careless about sowing corn, people of every age having gone to the war’ (Annals 14.38). This suggests that the revolt’s cost to the Britons did not end with their defeat: even peoples who had only wavered were punished, and, with too little corn sown, there was little harvest to fall back on. Yet this is the account of a Roman senator writing about 55 years later, who explains the famine by the Britons’ own carelessness and does not ask whether the ravaging of their land made it worse; no British account survives to say how many suffered. Even so, on Tacitus’s evidence the Celts bore the heaviest immediate consequences of the revolt, because they faced both punishment and hunger after their defeat.

Checked against our checklist

Judge: a judgement (topic) sentence that answers the question and is the thesis’s first reason (most severe)

Evidence: a signal phrase (Tacitus reports), his exact words, short and embedded, and a reference

Analyse: what the evidence shows, with a relating verb (suggests), and how it supports the Judge

Evaluate: weighs the source: who wrote it, when, what he leaves out, and the gap no British account fills

Resolve: weighs and links back in the thesis’s degree (heaviest), sharper than the Judge

2 · An annotated exemplar · I do · 3 of 5: body paragraph 2 · two kinds of evidence, linked

Body paragraph 2: two sources, one argument

Assess the consequences of the revolt for the Celts and the Romans. Paragraph 2 argues the second reason in the preview.

A teacher’s exemplar · written for this lesson · body paragraph 2 · the Romans

For the Romans, the consequences were serious but short-lived, because the revolt cost Rome a governor, not the province. Tacitus reports that Nero sent ‘two thousand legionaries, eight cohorts of auxiliaries, and a thousand cavalry’ from Germany (Annals 14.38), and the new procurator, Julius Classicianus, whom his tombstone calls ‘procurator of the province of Britain’ (RIB 12), told Rome that the fighting would not end ‘unless Suetonius were superseded’ (14.38). Nero’s freedman Polyclitus was sent to inquire, and Suetonius was later ordered to hand over his army to Petronius Turpilianus (14.39). This sequence suggests that Rome answered the revolt by repairing its army and changing its leadership, not by giving up Britain: the crisis became a quarrel between two officials rather than a lasting loss of power. However, Tacitus takes the governor’s side, blaming Classicianus for letting ‘private animosities interfere with the public interest’ (14.38), so the quarrel may be sharpened in his account; and the tombstone confirms Classicianus’s name and post, but not his policy. On balance, Rome’s immediate consequences were a reinforced army and a change of governor, costs it could bear, so they weighed far less than those the Celts suffered.

Checked against our checklist

Judge: the second reason in the preview, in the same order and words (a governor, not the province)

Evidence: two kinds of source, a historian with a signal phrase and an inscription, each with its reference

Analyse with cohesion: This sequence sums up the evidence in a summary noun, then says what it shows

Evaluate: an attribution that weighs (takes the governor’s side, blaming …), and what the tombstone cannot show

Resolve: weighs Rome’s costs against the Celts’ and links back to the thesis (far less)

2 · An annotated exemplar · I do · 4 of 5: body paragraph 3 · how sure can we be?

Body paragraph 3: modality matched to the evidence

Assess the consequences of the revolt for the Celts and the Romans. Paragraph 3 argues the third reason in the preview.

A teacher’s exemplar · written for this lesson · body paragraph 3 · the longer term

In the longer term, the revolt probably changed how Britain was ruled and defended more than who ruled it, although these consequences are harder to prove. In the Agricola, Tacitus says that Turpilianus was sent ‘to initiate a milder rule’ and that his successor ‘controlled the province by a certain courtesy in his administration’ (16), while in the Annals he calls Turpilianus’s peace ‘tame inaction’ (14.39). At Camulodunum, town walls dated to about 65–80 CE were probably built soon after the revolt and ‘presumably as a consequence of it’ (Victoria County History, 1994), and the Fison Way enclosure in Iceni country was dismantled in the 60s or 70s CE. Together, this evidence suggests that both sides lived with the revolt’s legacy: Britons under a gentler government, and Romans behind new defences. Yet only one link is reported: Tacitus ties the milder rule to the revolt’s aftermath, but he puts the later inaction down to a governor ‘who was somewhat indolent’ and to the civil war (Agricola 16), the walls’ link rests on the historians’ presumably, and no record says why Fison Way was dismantled. Therefore, the revolt’s long-term consequences were real but uncertain in size, and they changed the style of Roman rule rather than ending it.

Checked against our checklist

Judge: modality from the first sentence (probably), with a concession (harder to prove)

Evidence: three kinds together: one writer’s two works, a county history, an excavation record

Analyse: Together draws the evidence into one claim about both sides

Evaluate: report or inference? Each link gets the modality it deserves (only one is reported; presumably; no record says why)

Resolve: a judgement of size (real but uncertain), linked back to the thesis

2 · An annotated exemplar · I do · 5 of 5: the conclusion · then cold call

The conclusion: end on a judgement

Assess the consequences of the revolt for the Celts and the Romans.

A teacher’s exemplar · written for this lesson · the conclusion

Ultimately, the revolt’s consequences were far heavier for the Celts than for the Romans. The Britons who had fought, and some who had only wavered, faced punishment and famine, while Rome’s losses ended in a reinforced army, a change of governor and a milder policy. That milder rule and the new walls mattered to both sides, but the walls’ link to the revolt is inferred and the milder rule changed the style of Roman rule, not who held power, so they cannot outweigh the punishment and famine that Tacitus reports. The Celts paid for the revolt with punishment and hunger; Rome paid with a governor, and kept its province.

Which sentence of the conclusion could you delete and lose the most? Why?

The last. It is the decisive judgement on both sides, in the thesis’s degree; without it the conclusion would end on a summary. The other sentences lead up to it.

What it does

Thesis refined: the same degree as the introduction (far heavier … than)

Reasons drawn together, in the paragraphs’ order: no new evidence

Weigh: why the longer-term consequences, less certain or less severe, cannot outweigh the reported ones

A decisive final sentence: a judgement on both sides, not a summary of what the essay did

  2. 2Tacitus gives about 230,000 as the size of the British army in the final battle. False — that figure is Dio’s (62.8); Tacitus writes only that ‘some indeed say’ almost 80,000 Britons fell, with about 400 Romans (Annals 14.37).
  3. 3William Cowper’s ode about Boadicea (1782) is a Victorian representation. False — 1782 is in the Georgian period; Victoria reigned from 1837 to 1901.
  4. 4Colchester’s burnt layer proves who set the town on fire. False — it shows a great fire consistent with 60 or 61 CE, but not who lit it, why, or how many died; the link to the revolt comes from Tacitus.
  5. 5A thesis that previews the argument needs a separate sentence announcing what the essay will do. False — the thesis itself names the arguments, in order; no announcing sentence is needed.
  6. 6Assess and evaluate both ask for a judgement. True — NESA: assess, ‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’; evaluate, ‘Make a judgement based on criteria.’

Practice and review · 2 of 5 · re-teach cards · read the card for each dot point you missed

Re-teach cards: the hardest items

Who was who · dot points 3 and 9

Give the job of Suetonius Paulinus, Decianus Catus, Julius Classicianus, Polyclitus and Petronius Turpilianus.

Suetonius Paulinus: the governor (army commander and chief judge). Decianus Catus: the procurator (finances and taxes), who fled to Gaul. Julius Classicianus: the new procurator. Polyclitus: Nero’s freedman, sent to inquire. Petronius Turpilianus: the next governor. Never call Catus “governor”.

Numbers are claims · dot point 5

Which writer gives which number?

Tacitus: “About seventy thousand” dead in the towns (14.33: his own hedge is ‘About’; the translators’ ‘it appeared’ renders the Latin constitit, ‘it was established’); ‘some indeed say’ almost 80,000 Britons and 400 Romans fell in the battle (14.37). Dio: 80,000 Romans and allies (62.1); some 120,000 assembled (62.2); about 230,000 at the battle (62.8). Round numbers, written long after: claims, not counts.

Where Tacitus and Dio disagree · dot points 7 and 8

Who decided when to fight? Were prisoners taken? How did she die?

The battle: Tacitus says Suetonius decided to fight and chose the ground (14.34); Dio says he was ‘compelled, contrary to his judgment’, to fight when he did (62.8). Prisoners: Tacitus says the rebels were not bent ‘on making prisoners’ (14.33); Dio says some were taken captive (62.7). Her death: poison (Tacitus, 14.37) or illness (Dio, 62.12). Always say who says what.

What the ground can show · dot point 4

What can the archaeology of the revolt show, and how sure can we be?

Burnt layers at Colchester and London: a great fire, dated by coins and pottery in undisturbed layers to a time consistent with 60 or 61 CE; not who, why or how many. The Fenwick hoard: “It seems likely” it was hidden for safe-keeping. Longinus’s tombstone: the re-excavators’ evidence strongly suggests its face broke off after it fell, so ‘defaced’ is doubtful. Facilis: ‘probable’ that it fell in the revolt. Fison Way: dismantled in the 60s or 70s; a link to the revolt is possible, not proven.

The writers · dot points 5 and 6

When did Tacitus and Dio write, and whose figure is ‘40,000,000 sesterces’?

Tacitus (about 55–120 CE): the Annals, after about 113 CE, about 55 years after the revolt; his father-in-law Agricola served under Suetonius. Dio: about 150 years after; Book 62 survives mainly in a later summary (an epitome). The 40,000,000 sesterces of Seneca’s loan is Cary’s conversion: Dio’s Greek says ten million and names no coin. The date: 60 or 61 CE (Tacitus’s consuls give 61; some historians put the events in 60).

Georgian or Victorian? · dot point 10

Sort these by period: Cowper’s ode, Dickens’s history, Tennyson’s poem, Thornycroft’s group.

Georgian (1714–1830): Cowper’s ‘Boadicea: An Ode’ (1782). Victorian (Victoria’s reign, 1837–1901): Dickens, A Child’s History of England (1851); Tennyson’s ‘Boädicéa’ (1864); Thornycroft’s group, begun 1856 but installed in 1902, the year after Victoria died.

Practice and review · 4 of 5 · extension · argue the other side

Were Rome’s losses greater than the exemplar says?

Exercise 3 · one body paragraph

The exemplar judges the consequences far lighter for Rome. Write a counter-paragraph arguing that the revolt’s consequences for Rome were greater than they first appear. Use five moves and only evidence from Lessons 7–11.

Start from

Dio, Roman History 62.1 · Annals 14.38–39 · Agricola 16

A model paragraph · written for this lesson

The revolt’s consequences for Rome were greater than its recovery suggests, because the crisis forced Rome to change both its army in Britain and its policy. Dio, writing about 150 years later, claims that ‘the island was lost to Rome’ (62.1), and Nero had to send ‘two thousand legionaries, eight cohorts of auxiliaries, and a thousand cavalry’ from Germany (Annals 14.38). Turpilianus was then sent ‘to initiate a milder rule’ (Agricola 16), which suggests that Rome changed how it governed Britain, not only who governed it. Yet Dio’s ‘lost’ describes the crisis at its worst, not its result, since Suetonius went on to win the final battle and Rome kept the province. On balance, the revolt cost Rome more than a governor, but much less than a province.

Practice and review · 5 of 5 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. Which directive verbs ask for a judgement, and what does NESA say assess means? Assess, evaluate and to what extent (not a glossary word). Assess: ‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’
  2. How do describe and outline differ from explain? Describe and outline show what (features; the main points, briefly); explain shows how and why, with cause and effect.
  3. What must a thesis that previews the argument do? Answer the question with a degree and name the arguments in the order the body paragraphs make them, with no separate announcing sentence.
  4. What does a decisive conclusion do? Refines the thesis in the same degree, draws the reasons together with no new evidence, and ends on a judgement, not a summary.
  5. Who replaced Decianus Catus, and who replaced Suetonius Paulinus? Julius Classicianus became procurator; Petronius Turpilianus became governor.
  6. Which numbers are Tacitus’s, and which are Dio’s? Tacitus: about 70,000 in the towns; ‘some indeed say’ almost 80,000 Britons and 400 Romans in the battle. Dio: 80,000 Romans and allies; about 230,000 Britons at the battle.
