Unit 6 · Slavery in ancient Rome · Lesson 7

Owners’ attitudes: Cato, Varro, Columella and Seneca

Four Roman owners wrote about the people they enslaved. How hard or how humane were they, and why did their attitudes differ?

Year 11 Ancient History · Present with → or space · Read on a phone with R

Review · books closed · 6 minutes · press K for the timer

Five questions on law and enslaved people, and one thesis to fix

  1. What does the jurist Florentinus say slavery is (Digest 1.5.4.1)? ‘a creation of the jus gentium, by which a man is subjected, contrary to nature, to ownership on the part of another’. Florentinus was a jurist of the 2nd–3rd centuries CE; his words survive in the Digest, which was compiled for the emperor Justinian and published in 533 CE.
  2. What was a peculium? A fund of property or money that the master made available to an enslaved person for the conduct of business. Gardner (2011, p. 421): it ‘remained his property’.
  3. What did the aediles’ edict require of a sale ticket (Gellius 4.2.1)? That it show ‘exactly what disease or defect each one has, which one is a runaway or a vagabond’ (the edict as Gellius quotes it): a rule that protected the buyer, an owner.
  4. What two limits on owners does Gaius attribute to Antoninus Pius (Institutes 1.53)? An owner who kills his own enslaved person sine causa (“without cause”) is as liable to punishment as a man who kills another’s; and on proof of ‘intolerable cruelty’ an owner could be compelled to sell the people he enslaved.
  5. Why can law not show us how owners treated enslaved people day to day? Law records what jurists and emperors ruled, and what worried owners, not what happened in households. The Digest, published in 533 CE, was compiled from the works of jurists, especially of the late 2nd and early 3rd centuries CE (Gardner 2011, p. 414), so it is also later than most of what it reports.
  6. Writing · make this thesis arguable: “Enslaved people did many different jobs.” Two tests (Lesson 5): could someone disagree? does it name its reasons? For example: Although the sources show mines and farms most clearly, enslaved people probably did almost every kind of work in Roman life, largely because owners put them to whatever work paid or served them.

1 · Cato and Varro · I do — think aloud · Cato’s handbook for owners · 2 minutes

Cato’s advice for the owner’s first visit: what is to be sold?

Source · a farming handbook, on what an owner should sell

Sell worn-out oxen, blemished cattle, blemished sheep, wool, hides, an old wagon, old tools, an old slave, a sickly slave, and whatever else is superfluous. The master should have the selling habit, not the buying habit.

Cato, De Agri Cultura 2.7, about 160 BCE, advice to a farm owner (Cato, 234–149 BCE, was a Roman senator) · trans. W. D. Hooper and H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1934)

  1. 1Who wrote it, and for whomAn owner, advising owners on running a farm.
  2. 2What is on the listOxen, cattle, sheep, wool, hides, an old wagon, old tools … and “an old slave” and “a sickly slave”, in the same sentence.
  3. 3What the owner should beSomeone with “the selling habit, not the buying habit”.
  4. 4What to be careful ofThis is one passage, in a handbook about running a farm. It shows what Cato wrote to owners here, not everything he thought.

How does Cato treat an old or sick enslaved person here, and what does that show about how he sees them?

He lists them with worn-out oxen and old tools as things to sell, so what matters to him is whether they are still useful to the farm. In this passage he sees them as part of the farm’s stock, and says nothing about them as people.

1 · Cato and Varro · I do — think aloud · a speaker in a dialogue reports a scheme · 2 minutes

Varro’s dialogue: three classes of “instruments”

Source · a farming dialogue, on the means of tilling the land

Some divide these into two parts: men, and those aids to men without which they cannot cultivate; others into three: the class of instruments which is articulate, the inarticulate, and the mute; the articulate comprising the slaves, the inarticulate comprising the cattle, and the mute comprising the vehicles.

All agriculture is carried on by men — slaves, or freemen, or both;

Varro, De Re Rustica 1.17.1–2, 37 BCE, a dialogue about farming that Varro addressed to his wife Fundania, who had bought an estate (the speaker is Scrofa; Varro, 116–27 BCE, was a Roman scholar) · trans. W. D. Hooper and H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1934)

  1. 1Whose scheme is it?A speaker in the dialogue reports what others do: some divide the means of farming in two, others in three.
  2. 2What does the three-part scheme do?It puts enslaved people (“the articulate”) in the same list as cattle and carts: three kinds of “instruments”.
  3. 3What does the speaker do next?He goes straight on, calling the workers “men — slaves, or freemen, or both”.

What does this show that owners could say, and what does it not show about Varro?

It shows that some people could class enslaved people as a kind of farm equipment, and that no one in the dialogue objects at this point. It does not show that Varro held the scheme himself: a speaker reports it as what ‘others’ do, and then calls the workers ‘men’.

1 · Cato and Varro · I do — think aloud · the same speaker, advice on treatment · 1½ minutes

The same speaker on treating enslaved workers: what is advised, and why?

Source · the same dialogue, on foremen and hands

The good will of the foremen should be won by treating them with some degree of consideration; and those of the hands who excel the others should also be consulted as to the work to be done.

They are made to take more interest in their work by being treated more liberally in respect either of food, or of more clothing, or of exemption from work, or of permission to graze some cattle of their own on the farm, or other things of this kind; so that, if some unusually heavy task is imposed, or punishment inflicted on them in some way, their loyalty and kindly feeling to the master may be restored by the consolation derived from such measures.

Varro, De Re Rustica 1.17.6–7, 37 BCE (the speaker is Scrofa; 1.17.5, on property and mates, was Lesson 4’s source) · trans. W. D. Hooper and H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1934)

  1. 1What is advised“Some degree of consideration”; consulting the best hands; more food, clothing, exemption from work, and cattle of their own.
  2. 2What the owner is to gainForemen’s “good will”; more interest in the work; and “loyalty and kindly feeling to the master” restored after a heavy task or punishment.
  3. 3What is taken for grantedThat heavy tasks will be imposed and that punishment will be inflicted.

What does the speaker advise, and what does he want in return?

He advises ‘some degree of consideration’, better food and clothing and some freedom from work, so that the workers’ ‘loyalty and kindly feeling to the master’ is kept or restored. The kindness has a purpose, the master’s gain, and the advice takes punishment for granted.

2 · Columella and Seneca · We do — supported reading · 3 minutes · underline what he allows, circle what he gains, box the chains

Columella: talking and jesting with workers, and an underground prison

Source · a farming handbook, on the owner’s own practice

In the case of the other slaves, the following are, in general, the precepts to be observed, and I do not regret having held to them myself: to talk rather familiarly with the country slaves, provided only that they have not conducted themselves unbecomingly, more frequently than I would with the town slaves; and when I perceived that their unending toil was lightened by such friendliness on the part of the master, I would even jest with them at times and allow them also to jest more freely. Nowadays I make it a practice to call them into consultation on any new work, as if they were more experienced, and to discover by this means what sort of ability is possessed by each of them and how intelligent he is. Furthermore, I observe that they are more willing to set about a piece of work on which they think that their opinions have been asked and their advice followed.

Columella, De Re Rustica 1.8.15, mid-1st century CE, an owner describing the precepts he follows himself · trans. H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1941)

Source · the same handbook, on the farm buildings

It will be best that cubicles for unfettered slaves be built to admit the midday sun at the equinox; for those who are in chains there should be an underground prison, as wholesome as possible, receiving light through a number of narrow windows built so high from the ground that they cannot be reached with the hand.

Columella, De Re Rustica 1.6.3, mid-1st century CE · trans. H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1941)

What does Columella say he allows the country workers, and what does he say he gains?

He talks familiarly with them, jests with them and consults them. He says their toil was ‘lightened by such friendliness’ and that they are ‘more willing to set about a piece of work’ when their opinions are asked: so the friendliness also gains him willing work.

What does 1.6.3 add to the picture?

In the same handbook he plans an ‘underground prison’ for those in chains, with windows too high to reach. So one owner’s handbook shows friendly talk for workers who behave, and chains and a prison for others: one attitude cannot be read from one sentence.

2 · Columella and Seneca · We do — supported reading · 3 minutes · underline each reason he gives owners

Seneca’s Epistle 47: what reasons does he give owners?

Source · a letter, 47.5

They are not enemies when we acquire them; we make them enemies.

Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 47.5, about 62–65 CE · trans. R. M. Gummere (Loeb, 1917)

Source · the same letter, 47.10

Kindly remember that he whom you call your slave sprang from the same stock, is smiled upon by the same skies, and on equal terms with yourself breathes, lives, and dies. It is just as possible for you to see in him a free-born man as for him to see in you a slave.

Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 47.10 · trans. R. M. Gummere (Loeb, 1917)

Source · the same letter, 47.11

Treat your inferiors as you would be treated by your betters. And as often as you reflect how much power you have over a slave, remember that your master has just as much power over you.

Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 47.11 · trans. R. M. Gummere (Loeb, 1917)

What reasons does Seneca give owners for treating enslaved people better?

Three: harsh treatment turns them into enemies (‘we make them enemies’); a free man could be brought low and enslaved (‘as possible for you to see in him a free-born man as for him to see in you a slave’); and an owner is himself under a master’s power (‘your master has just as much power over you’). Some of the reasons appeal to the owner’s own interest.

Whose words do we not have?

The enslaved people’s. Seneca says they ‘talk about their master’ (47.4) but gives none of their words, and he writes to an owner. We see their treatment only through an owner’s eyes.

2 · Columella and Seneca · We do — cold call, then a historian’s judgement · 2 minutes

Is Seneca against slavery?

Cold call: yes, no or partly? Which of his words decide it?

Look back at

47.1 “Nay, rather they are men”; 47.5 “we make them enemies”; 47.11 “Treat your inferiors as you would be treated by your betters”.

A historian · on what Seneca did and did not call for

… the most liberal set of doctrines on slavery that had ever been articulated at Rome—as far, that is, as the extant corpus of ancient literature allows of judgment.

But Seneca did not reach the point of calling for an end to slavery as the logical outcome of his thought, and all that he sought from slaves themselves was a respectful, loving attitude toward their masters.

K. R. Bradley, Slavery and Rebellion in the Roman World, 140 B.C.–70 B.C. (Indiana University Press, 1989), p. 102. “Slaves” is his word.

So where does Seneca sit, and why a line rather than for and against?

At the humane end of owners’ attitudes, but not outside them: he asks owners to treat enslaved people better, and, Bradley argues, he did not call for slavery to end. All four writers were owners, so the useful question is how hard or how humane each asks an owner to be, not whether he is for or against slavery.

3 · You do · Writing focus · 7 minutes · I do → We do → apply

Writing focus: attribute the view, then explain it

I do · watch

The rule in one line: to account for a perspective, first attribute the view to its writer, then explain it from his context and purpose: who, when, for whom, why.

Worked example · written for this lesson · on Seneca, who is not one of the writers you compare

Seneca, a Stoic philosopher who had been Nero’s tutor and was himself an owner, writing to his friend Lucilius in about 62–65 CE, urges owners to treat enslaved people kindly because, in his view, Fortune has equal rights over free and enslaved people alike; yet, as Bradley (1989) argues, he stopped short of calling for an end to slavery.

Who: his position, with the part that matters for this view

When and for whom: a date and an audience

The view, attributed: a reporting verb (‘urges’), not ‘proves’

Why: his own reason, marked ‘in his view’ (47.1, 47.10)

The limit: a historian’s judgement, attributed, in the present tense

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1Cato 2.7 lists “an old slave, a sickly slave” in the same sentence as worn-out oxen and old tools. True — it is a list of things an owner should sell; it shows what the passage says, not all that Cato thought.
  2. 2In his own voice, Varro says that enslaved people are “the articulate” instruments of farming. False — at 1.17.1 a speaker in his dialogue reports a scheme that ‘others’ use; Varro is not shown to hold it, and the speaker then calls the workers ‘men’.

Practice and review · 3 of 4 · extension

Do Bradley and Joshel read Epistle 47 fairly?

Exercise 3 · a short paragraph

Bradley says Seneca sought from enslaved people ‘a respectful, loving attitude toward their masters’; Joshel says his pervasive use of slavery as a metaphor ‘drains social realities of meaning’. Assess whether Epistle 47 supports these readings. Read the whole of Epistle 47 first (Wikisource: see Sources and credits).

A historian · on the metaphor

… Seneca’s pervasive use of slavery as a metaphor for the human condition and for elite subjects living under an emperor drains social realities of meaning.

S. R. Joshel, “Slavery and Roman literary culture”, in K. Bradley and P. Cartledge (eds), The Cambridge World History of Slavery, vol. 1 (Cambridge University Press, 2011), p. 234

The letter · parts of Epistle 47 for your evidence

… he cannot get away from his boyhood; he is dragged back to it; … and he must remain awake throughout the night …

Show me a man who is not a slave; one is a slave to lust, another to greed, another to ambition, and all men are slaves to fear.

… because I bid slaves respect their masters instead of fearing them. … Respect means love, and love and fear cannot be mingled.

Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 47.7 (the wine-server), 47.17 and 47.18 · trans. R. M. Gummere (Loeb, 1917)

A model · written for this lesson

Bradley’s reading fits Epistle 47 closely: Seneca says he bids enslaved people ‘respect their masters instead of fearing them’ and adds that ‘Respect means love’ (47.18), and nothing in the letter asks owners to free anyone. Joshel’s claim fits only in part. Epistle 47 does use slavery as a metaphor (‘one is a slave to lust, another to greed’, 47.17), which supports her point that what counts is the aristocrat’s own mind, but the same letter dwells on social realities, such as the cup-bearer kept boyish and awake all night (47.7), so here the realities are not drained away. Joshel writes of Seneca’s ‘pervasive’ practice across his works, and one letter cannot test that, so Epistle 47 supports Bradley more fully than it supports Joshel.
