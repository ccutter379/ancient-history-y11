First, one thing to fix from the last review

1 · Berenice II’s lock

Which poem tells the story, who wrote it, and what can’t it show?

Catullus 66, by the Roman poet Catullus (1st century BCE), adapting a Greek poem written for the Ptolemaic court (Callimachus’s Lock of Berenice): Conon saw “a lock of hair from Berenice’s head, in the celestial light” (Smithers). The vow of her hair and Conon’s naming of the constellation are usually accepted; the lock speaking and rising to the stars is the court poet’s compliment. It shows how a queen’s story was told, not what she did as queen.

2 · Cleopatra’s coin

What does the ANU bronze coin show, and what can’t it?

Bronze, minted at Alexandria, 44–30 BCE (the museum’s dating): her diademed portrait, an eagle on a thunderbolt, a double horn of plenty and the legend ‘of Queen Kleopatra’. The museum reads the double horn as co-regency with her son Caesarion. It can’t show how she ruled or how her subjects saw her: it is her own message.

3 · Phile of Priene

Who was she, and why is she an exception?

The first woman stephanephoros (chief magistrate) of Priene, a Greek city in Ionia (not Egypt), about 100–50 BCE; she paid for its reservoir and aqueducts from her own resources. An exception: Greek citizen women did not hold political office (priesthoods were religious offices), and one woman in one city can’t show that other women held office.

4 · The Canopus decree

Who passed it, when, and what does it do for Berenice’s daughter?

It is the hieroglyphic text of the decree of an assembly of Egyptian priests on 7 March 238 BCE (Egyptian evidence, about a Greek royal family). It deifies the dead princess Berenice: the priests vote her festivals and a divine image, and name the statue “Berenice, mistress of virgins.” It shows what the priests chose to honour.

5 · Writing: the same degree

A Judge says “only to a limited extent” and its Resolve, on the same criterion, says “largely”. Do they state the same degree?

No. The criterion is the same, but the degree changes: only to a limited extent, then largely. Fix the Resolve so that it repeats the Judge’s degree (Lesson 15: a Judge and a Resolve state the same degree and the same criterion).

Sources and their limits · dot point 1

Who made most sources about Greek women, and what is Lysias 1 useful for?

Almost all were made by men, and many for men (Lesson 1). Lysias, a speechwriter, wrote Lysias 1 for Euphiletus to speak in court. It is useful for how a husband wanted his household to look in court, not for his wife’s own view (Lesson 2).

Whose ideal? · dot point 2

Whose words are Thucydides 2.45.2 and Oeconomicus 7.22 and 7.30, and why do they show attitudes better than lives?

Pericles’s, as Thucydides reports him, and Ischomachus’s, as Socrates reports him in Xenophon’s dialogue. They are the ideals of elite men, each shaped by an audience and a purpose; no woman wrote any of them, and Xenophon’s indoor ideal relied on servants (Lesson 3).

Ideals in stone · dot point 3

What do the Dexileos and Hegeso stelai show, and why call them ideals rather than portraits?

Dexileos: a young cavalryman striking a fallen opponent; his inscription says he died at Corinth, and the museum says his relatives dedicated it. Hegeso: a seated woman with an attendant, in a family grave plot. Families probably chose the image: they show what a family wanted remembered, not what each person was like (Lesson 5).

Guardians, work and contracts · dot point 4

Who was a woman’s kyrios, and who was an epikleros? Why can one papyrus not show how all Greek women in Egypt lived?

A kyrios was her legal guardian, her father, then her husband, who acted for her in law. An epikleros was a daughter whose father died leaving no son (Lesson 6). A papyrus is one couple’s document, and the size of the dowry suggests a well-off couple; a historian needs more documents to say what was usual (Lesson 12).

Offices and exceptions · dot point 5

Which public offices in religion did some Athenian women hold, and what does Phile of Priene show?

Priesthoods: Myrrhine was chosen by lot from all, Lysimache by descent from a genos (Lesson 8). Phile of Priene, a Greek city in Ionia, is named as the first woman stephanephoros of her city: one woman in one city can’t show that other women held office (Lesson 16).

Aspasia and Arsinoe II · dot point 6

Which source on Aspasia is nearest her lifetime? What is the limit of the Met’s gold coin of Arsinoe II?

Aristophanes’s Acharnians, a comedy of 425 BCE, four years after Pericles died; Plutarch wrote about 100–120 CE and hedges (‘as some say’), and her trial may never have happened (Lesson 10). The Met dates the coin to about 261–246 BCE, after Arsinoe died in 270 or 268 BCE, so it shows how she was honoured and remembered more than what she did (Lesson 15).

Practice and review · 6 of 6 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What may you write or mark in reading time, and when do you mark the sources? Nothing: NESA’s 2027 HSC student guide says “You must not write on or mark your exam in any way or use any equipment (including highlighters).” You mark the sources and jot your plan in the first minutes of writing time.
  2. How do you work out minutes per mark? Writing minutes ÷ total marks (department advice). On our sample paper, 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5 a mark, so a 4-mark answer gets about 6 minutes. For the new-syllabus HSC paper (first set in 2028 — the one you will sit), NESA says “3 hours plus 10 minutes reading time” and 100 marks, so 180 ÷ 100 = 1.8 on average.
  3. Which verbs ask for a judgement, and what do NESA’s definitions say? Assess: ‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’ Evaluate: ‘Make a judgement based on criteria. Determine the value of.’ And to what extent, which NESA calls self-explanatory: a judgement of degree (department advice).
  4. What are the four steps of deconstructing a question? Verb · topic · limits · what your answer must do.
  5. What must a thesis of degree include, and what must it not include? A degree on the question’s own words and its main reasons, with a concession; no sentence announcing the essay.
  6. What is a sound order of attack on a whole paper? Reading time for thinking only; in the first minutes of writing, mark the sources and plan the extended response; short answers to their minutes; the extended response; a last check (department advice).
