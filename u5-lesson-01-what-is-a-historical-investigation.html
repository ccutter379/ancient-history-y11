What is a historical investigation? From topic to question

How do you turn something that interests you into a question that the evidence can answer, and that two people could answer differently?

Year 11 Ancient History · Present with → or space · Read on a phone with R

Boudicca again: how far?

Everyone · one sentence

Answer in one sentence, with a degree of extent: How far did the treatment of Boudicca’s family cause the revolt of 60 or 61 CE?

entirelylargelyto a significant extentpartlyonly to a limited extentnot at all
A strong judgement sentence

answers the question with a degree of extent, gives a reason with evidence, and names a limit (but, although). Any degree can be right if the evidence supports it.

One model · written for this lesson

The treatment of Boudicca’s family only partly caused the revolt of 60 or 61 CE: it helps explain why the Iceni rose, but Tacitus reports that the rebels’ hatred was most intense against the veterans of the colony at Camulodunum.

Today: every investigation question asks for a judgement like this one.

1 · What is an investigation? · I do — then cold call

A historical investigation: your question, your sources, your conclusion

  1. 1Your own questionYou choose a question about the ancient past, one the evidence could answer in more than one way.
  2. 2Sources, found and judgedYou find ancient and modern sources yourself, and judge each one for your question (OVPRL).
  3. 3A conclusion you can defendYou reach an answer, write it up with references, and explain how you know.

How is an investigation different from a project that collects facts about a topic?

An investigation answers a question with a judgement: it weighs the evidence and reaches a conclusion that someone else could dispute. A fact project collects information on a topic but never has to decide anything.

Source · the syllabus on the investigation

The Historical investigation is designed to provide opportunities for all students to develop the historical skills that are the core of the historical inquiry process.

NESA, Ancient History 11–12 Syllabus (2024), p. 46

The process, in the syllabus’s words
  1. Formulate historical questions and hypotheses relevant to the investigation
  2. Identify and analyse sources to develop a view about a historical issue
  3. Develop and/or examine historical interpretations
  4. Synthesise evidence and information from a range of sources to develop and support a reasoned historical account or argument
  5. Use historical terms and concepts appropriately
  6. Present and communicate the findings of a historical investigation using appropriate and well-structured forms

1 · What is an investigation? · I do — where a topic can come from · then cold call

Where can a topic come from? The syllabus lists eight areas

  1. aspects of an ancient society as revealed through sourcesMet this year: what the Fortification Tablets reveal about Persepolis
  2. the causes and impacts of a historical eventMet this year: why the revolt of 60 or 61 CE happened
  3. the role of a significant historical individualMet this year: the roles of Boudicca and Suetonius Paulinus
  4. the significance of a historical developmentMet this year: Darius reorganises the satrapies and fixes tribute
  5. tracing the development of an aspect of the ancient past over time through a thematic approachMet this year: royal building from Pasargadae to Persepolis
  6. the analysis of a historical debate, controversy or representation of the pastMet this year: was the burning of Persepolis deliberate or drunken?
  7. the contribution of a historical site or archaeological evidence to our understanding of the pastMet this year: what the ruins of Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium show
  8. an interpretation or representation of an individual, group or eventMet this year: Boudicca in the Victorian period

Boudicca’s revolt appears under areas 2, 3, 7 and 8. How can one topic fit more than one area?

The question decides the area, not the topic. Why did the revolt happen? is causes (2); How important was Boudicca’s leadership? is an individual’s role (3); What do the ruined towns show about the revolt? is a site’s contribution (7); Why did the Victorians make her a heroine? is a representation (8).

1 · What is an investigation? · I do — climbing the question ladder aloud

Climb the question ladder: from an interest to a question

  1. 1Interest · the pyramidsSomething you want to know about. Far too big: hundreds of years and many pyramids.
  2. 2Topic · who built the Great PyramidNarrower: one pyramid, one issue. But still not a question.
  3. 3Descriptive question · How were the pyramids built?Now a question, but it covers many pyramids, and as asked it invites a description of methods, not a judgement on a claim.
  4. 4Investigation question · How far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?One pyramid and one ancient claim, tested against other evidence. Two people could reach different answers.
Three tests for every rung
  • Arguable: could two people reasonably answer it differently, using the evidence?
  • Focused: one place, one time, one issue: small enough to answer well?
  • Answerable from sources: are there sources you can find and read?
And one rule · Year 12 is off limits

The investigation must not overlap with or duplicate significantly any option to be attempted in the Year 12 Ancient History or History Extension courses.

NESA, Ancient History 11–12 Syllabus (2024), p. 46

1 · What is an investigation? · I do — the question we will model all unit · then cold call

Our class investigation, modelled every lesson

The class question · closed: no one may choose itHow far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?

Source · Herodotus on King Khufu (Kheops)

… Kheops, who was the next king, brought the people to utter misery.

… he compelled all the Egyptians to work for him.

They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months.

Herodotus, Histories 2.124 · a Greek writer, about 430 BCE, more than 2,000 years after Khufu · he begins They said: he reports what Egyptians told him · trans. A. D. Godley (Loeb, 1920), as revised by the Perseus Digital Library

Read exactly: this translation says compelled; it does not say slaves. A later lesson looks at the Greek word behind compelled. The bust is a Roman copy, 2nd century CE, of a Greek statue probably made decades after Herodotus died: probably not a likeness from life.

Images: The Met (CC0 1.0). Full details on the last slide.

The evidence we will test it against
  • Herodotus himself: who told him, and when he wrote.
  • The builders’ town at Giza (Heit el-Ghurab), dug since 1988 by Ancient Egypt Research Associates (AERA): bakeries, kitchens, workshops. The excavators link it to kings ‘Menkaure, Khafre, and probably Khufu’.
  • Papyri from Wadi al-Jarf, found in 2013: the journal of an official named Merer, from late in Khufu’s reign, published by the excavator, Pierre Tallet.

Why is this a good investigation question? Use the three tests.

Arguable: Herodotus and the excavated evidence can be weighed differently, so two people could reach different degrees of how far. Focused: one pyramid, one account. Answerable: a translated text, an excavation, published papyri.

2 · From topic to question · We do — sort in pairs · then cold call

Sort eight questions: investigable, needs work, or not a question?

#CardSort · and why
1Ancient EgyptNot a question. A whole civilisation over thousands of years: no time, place or question word.
2Why did the people of Teotihuacan build the Temple of the Feathered Serpent?Investigable. One building whose uses are debated, with the excavated temple as evidence.
3Who was Sargon of Akkad?Needs work. It asks for a description of one man’s life, so two people would give much the same answer.

2 · From topic to question · We do — repair in pairs · then compare

Repair two: climb the ladder from the rung they are stuck on

Narrow it (time? place? people?) · choose a topic area · open it with How far, Why, How significant or To what extent · test it.

Repair 1 · Ancient Egypt
  1. Ancient Egypt
  2. Old Kingdom Egypt: the age of the Giza pyramids
  3. the end of the Old Kingdom
  4. How far did changes in climate cause the collapse of Old Kingdom Egypt?

Area 2 (causes) or area 6 (a debate): historians argue over climate and internal divisions. Arguable, focused, answerable.

Repair 2 · Who was Sargon of Akkad?
  1. Who was Sargon of Akkad? (a description)
  2. Sargon as later people remembered him
  3. How far do later legends about Sargon of Akkad match the evidence from his own reign?

Area 8 (a representation of an individual): legends against the evidence of his time. Arguable and focused; answerable, though many of his own inscriptions survive only in later copies, a limit to name.

Any repair that passes the three tests is a good one: these are not the only answers.

3 · You do · Writing focus · 7 minutes · I do → We do → apply

Writing focus: deconstruct the question before you research it

I do · watch

Investigate: ‘Plan, inquire into and draw conclusions about.’ Propose: ‘Put forward … for consideration or action.’ (NESA, Glossary of key words) Investigating is the whole process; proposing an answer to test is one step in it. Then each question makes its own demand:

How far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?

Verb
how far: a judgement of degree, like to what extent (a self-explanatory term, not a key word)
Topic
who built the Great Pyramid, as Herodotus tells it
Limits
one pyramid, Khufu’s; Herodotus’s account against other evidence
Do
weigh his claim and say how far it holds

Worked example · written for this lesson

“How far” asks me for a judgement of degree, so my answer must weigh Herodotus’s claim against the other evidence and say how far it holds, not describe the pyramid.

We do · together

The class practice question (closed): Why did the Assyrian Empire collapse in the late 7th century BCE?

Verb
why: explain the causes; the closest glossary verb is explain: ‘Relate cause and effect.’
Topic
the collapse of the Assyrian Empire
Limits
the late 7th century BCE; the collapse, not the empire’s whole history
Do
explain several causes, show how they link, and judge which mattered most
How the two questions differ

How far asks for a degree of support for one claim; why asks for causes, ranked. Both ask for a judgement, so both are investigable.

A model summary · written for this lesson

A historical investigation is an inquiry into a question of your own: you find and judge the sources, then reach and present a conclusion you can defend. A topic can come from any of the syllabus’s eight areas, from the causes of an event to a debate or a representation, but it becomes an investigation only when it is turned into a question that asks for a judgement. A strong question is arguable, focused and answerable from sources, and clear of Year 12, like our class question: how far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?

Practice and review · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A historical investigation collects as many facts as possible about a topic. False — it answers a question with a judgement, using sources you find and judge, and reaches a conclusion you can defend.
  2. 2Ancient Rome is a good investigation question. False — it is a topic, not a question: it needs a time, a place, an issue and a question word.
  3. 3A question that begins Did …? usually needs work. True — it invites yes or no; How far …? or To what extent …? opens it up.
  4. 4Herodotus says that slaves built the Great Pyramid. False — in Godley’s translation he reports what Egyptians told him: that Kheops (Khufu) ‘compelled all the Egyptians to work for him’, in gangs that served three months each; he does not say slaves.
  5. 5Your investigation may overlap with a Year 12 option if it interests you. False — the syllabus says it ‘must not overlap with or duplicate significantly’ any option you will study in Year 12 Ancient History or History Extension.
  6. 6One topic can fit more than one topic area. True — the question decides the area: why Boudicca’s revolt happened is causes; how important her leadership was is an individual’s role.
