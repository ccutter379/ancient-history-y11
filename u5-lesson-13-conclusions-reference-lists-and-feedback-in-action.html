Unit 5 · Historical investigation · Lesson 13

Conclusions, reference lists and feedback in action

How does a conclusion state its judgement and its limits, and how do you use feedback to improve what you wrote?

Year 11 Ancient History · Present with → or space · Read on a phone with R

Review · books closed · 6 minutes · press K for the timer

Five questions and a writing item

  1. A report has four parts. Name them, in order, and say which one holds the thesis. An introduction (the context, the question and the thesis); sections, each with a heading that states a claim; a conclusion; and a reference list. The thesis is in the introduction.
  2. What makes a heading a claim heading? It answers the sub-question, so a source could support or challenge it; a topic label only names a subject. It may be short if the topic sentence carries the hedge and the limit.
  3. What is writing from notes, and how is patchwriting different? Writing from notes means closing the source, writing a sentence from your own note, then checking it against the original. Patchwriting: swapping synonyms into a source’s sentence; still plagiarism. A pass has no run of four or more of the source’s words outside quotation marks (names, titles and numbers don’t count).
  4. Name three historical terms and give a one-line meaning of each. For example: provenance, where evidence comes from (who wrote or found it, and when); corroboration, support for a claim from an independent source; secondary source, a later work about the time studied.
  5. NESA defines investigate as ‘Plan, inquire into and draw conclusions about.’ What does an investigation therefore need? A question, sources and a conclusion that answers the question: the part we write today.
  6. Writing · which is the better heading, and why: ‘Section 2’ or ‘Fed at the crown’s expense’? ‘Fed at the crown’s expense’. It states a claim a source could support or challenge. ‘Section 2’ only gives a number: it names no topic, let alone a claim.

Mark your own answers in a different colour. Which question did you find hardest?

Words for conclusions and feedback

Writing termsMeaning
Concluding judgementThe final answer, with its degree and its limits; no new evidence.
Degree of extentHow far a judgement goes: entirely, largely, partly, only to a limited extent. It answers how far; it is not a hedge.
Hedge (modality)A word or phrase that matches a claim’s certainty to the evidence: probably, suggests, on present evidence.

1 · Whole-class feedback · 2 minutes · the three most common strengths

What worked in the section drafts

JA topic sentence with a degree and one hedge

Most topic sentences answered the sub-question with a degree and one hedge, so the reader knew the section’s answer at once.

Looks like“On present evidence, the town largely supports a picture of crews that the crown probably organised and fed, not of a people brought to ‘utter misery’, although it may belong mainly to later kings.”
EvSources named, with an accurate verb

Most sections named each source, chose a verb that fitted what it did, and cited it where it was used.

Looks like“Herodotus reports that Egyptians told him Khufu made work compulsory (Herodotus 2.124), whereas Lehner suggests that everybody owed a ‘labor tax’ (Lehner 1997).”
AA precise verb for what the sources show

Many drafts said what two sources show together, with suggest or indicate, not prove.

Looks like“Together, the royal silos and the bakeries suggest a crown that planned its crews’ food (Ancient Egypt Research Associates n.d.).”
Reading your feedback · our class checklist

Your draft comes back with one strength and one next step, each coded to a point on our checklist (Lesson 9).

  1. JJudge: answers the sub-question with a degree (largely, partly) and one hedge.
  2. EvEvidence: two or more sources, each named, attributed with an accurate verb and cited (author–date).
  3. AAnalyse: says what the sources show together, with a precise verb (suggests, indicates), never proves.
  4. EvlEvaluate: names what the evidence cannot show (a gap, a later date, one team, sources that are not independent).
  5. RResolve: a refined judgement at the same degree, with no new evidence.
  6. OOwn words: paraphrase, not patchwriting; present tense for what sources say, past tense for what happened.

Every example here and in the next steps is invented, written for this lesson, on the class question about Herodotus and the Great Pyramid. None is anyone’s real writing.

1 · Whole-class feedback · 4 minutes · the three most common next steps · before → after · we fix one live

Next steps: Evaluate, Resolve and Own words

Every Before is an invented sentence, written for this lesson, on the class question about Herodotus and the Great Pyramid.

EvlEvaluate: name a real limitwhat the evidence cannot show; weigh it, don’t dismiss it

BeforeThe Ministry’s page might be biased, so we can’t trust it.

AfterHowever, the Ministry places the town’s workers in the reigns of Khafre and Menkaure, so its evidence that the state kept them healthy may not apply to Khufu’s crews.

Why it works: it names the limit (a later date), says what it affects, and weighs the evidence instead of dismissing it.

RResolve: the same degree, no new evidencerefine the judgement; don’t change it or add to it

BeforeSo the town proves that Herodotus was wrong. (The section’s topic sentence said the town only points to organised, fed crews.)

AfterOn balance, then, the town points to crews that the crown organised and fed, although it may belong mainly to later kings.

Why it works: proves and wrong overclaim against the section’s own hedge; the after keeps the topic sentence’s hedge (points to), adds the limit and no new evidence.

OOwn words: write from your notenew words and a new structure, then cite

An enormous amount of fish, bird, cattle, sheep, goat, and pig bones were found, revealing that the state ensured that the workers were in good health …

Source · Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) n.d., ‘Workers’ Town and Cemetery’

BeforeA huge amount of fish, bird, cattle, sheep, goat and pig bones were discovered, showing that the state made sure the workers were in good health (Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) n.d.).

AfterThe Ministry reads the large quantity of animal bones from the town as a sign that the state kept its workers healthy (Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) n.d.).

Why it works: the before swaps synonyms into the source’s sentence, in its order: patchwriting. The after starts from who says it, changes the structure and attributes the reading to the Ministry.

2 · I do · the class model report’s conclusion, read and labelled · then cold call

The class model conclusion

How far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?

Conclusion

Herodotus’s account is therefore supported only in part. The evidence suggests that the crown organised and fed its crews, that their service was a duty owed to the king, and that the work was hard, but it does not show a people brought to “utter misery”. The judgement has limits: the town may belong mainly to later kings, Merer’s papyri describe one team, and on present evidence no worker has left a description of how the service felt, only an inspector’s work log.

The model report’s conclusion from Lesson 10 · written for that lesson. It adds no evidence, so it carries no citations: every claim was cited where it was argued.

The judgement, at the thesis’s degree: only in part. It may sharpen, but never contradict

The reasons, drawn together in the words of the argument: what the evidence suggests, and what it does not show

The limits, stated plainly, so the judgement is honest

Cold call: what is the same as the introduction’s thesis, and what is new?

The same: the degree (only in part), the reasons (crews organised and fed, a duty owed to the king, not “utter misery”) and the limit about the town’s date. New: one reason drawn from section 2 (the work was hard) and two more limits (one team; no worker’s description of how the service felt). No new evidence, so no new citations: each point was argued in a section.

2 · I do · name what could change the judgement · then cold call

Each limit points to the evidence that could change it

A limit the conclusion statesFurther evidence that could change itWhich way it would move the judgement
The town may belong mainly to later kingsA firm date for the town’s main buildingsIf they fall in Khufu’s reign, the judgement about his crews becomes firmer; if in later reigns, it becomes weaker.
Merer’s papyri describe one teamRecords from other teams, such as the crews who quarried and lifted stoneThey would show whether the other crews were organised and fed in the same way.
No worker has left a description of how the service feltA worker’s own description of the serviceIt could show how hard the service was to bear, and so how far “utter misery” fits.
Say it like this

‘Evidence that … would make this judgement firmer / weaker.’ · ‘Only … could show …’

Name the evidence and the way it would move the judgement. It is a limit turned into a test, not an apology.

Cold call: which limit is hardest to remove, and why?

The third. It needs a kind of source that may never have existed: the workers’ own descriptions. The first two need more excavation or a firmer date, which can happen. Some limits are gaps we can fill; some are silences we may not.

The check, both ways
  1. Every in-text citation has an entry: same name, same year (an ancient text has book and chapter, no year).
  2. Every entry is cited at least once in the text. Six sources cited, six entries.
  3. One style, author–date, in Lesson 6’s forms, in alphabetical order.

Cold call: a student’s list has a seventh entry that no sentence cites. What should they do?

Delete it, or cite it where they used it. The list holds only the sources cited in the text. A source read but not used stays on the annotated source list and in the log; one never read should not be listed at all.

3 · Feedback in action · Writing focus · 6 minutes · I do → We do → apply · on the class question

Writing focus: a concluding judgement with its limit

I do · watch

A concluding judgement gives a degree of extent, one hedge and a limit, in one sentence, with no new evidence.

Worked example · written for this lesson, on the class question

On present evidence, the Great Pyramid was more probably built by crews whom the crown organised and fed, and whose service was a duty owed to the king, than by a people brought to “utter misery”, so Herodotus is supported only in part; Merer’s log records what one team did, but only a worker’s own description of the service could show how it felt.

What it rests on: the sources so far

The hedge: more probably … than

The degree of extent, as in the thesis

The limit: what the evidence cannot show

We do · cold call

Look at the sentence. Which words carry the degree of extent, which the hedge, and which the limit?

Three jobs, three phrases

Degree of extent: supported only in part. Hedge: more probably … than. Limit: only a worker’s own description of the service could show how it felt: it names what the evidence cannot show. On present evidence says what the claim rests on.

Now find the degree of extent in your own thesis. Would your conclusion keep it?

Apply · in your own conclusion

Write your concluding judgement in one sentence, for your own question: a degree of extent, one hedge and one limit. Frame: ‘On present evidence, … more probably … than …, so [the claim tested] is supported [degree of extent]; only … could show …’ Adapt it to your question; keep the degree and the limit. 3 · Feedback in action · conclusion 10 minutes · then the reference-list check, 5 minutes · press K for the timer

Draft your conclusion, then check your reference list

Your conclusion · starts from your one-sentence judgement
  1. Answers your question with a refined judgement: your hypothesis confirmed, modified or rejected, in your own words.
  2. Keeps your thesis’s degree: it may sharpen, never contradict.
  3. Draws your sections together: the reasons, in the words of your argument.
  4. States the limits: what your evidence cannot show.
  5. Names the further evidence that could change it, and which way.
  6. Adds no new evidence and no new citation. It ends on the judgement and its limits, not on a summary of what your report did.
Support

Start your conclusion with the frame from the Writing focus, then add one sentence that begins ‘The judgement has limits: …’

Your reference-list checklist
  1. Every in-text citation in my draft has an entry in my list.
  2. Every entry is cited at least once in my text.
  3. The name and the year match in both places (book and chapter for an ancient text).
  4. Entries are in alphabetical order by author.
  5. Each entry follows Lesson 6’s forms: who · when · what · where · find it again.
  6. Web pages have a viewed date; articles have a DOI; no date means n.d.
Extension

Name the one piece of evidence that would most change your conclusion, explain why it does not survive or has not been found, and fold that into your final sentence.

A model summary · written for this lesson

A concluding judgement answers the question at the degree of the thesis, draws the reasons together, states the limits and names the evidence that could change it, and it adds no new evidence and does not contradict the thesis. I check a reference list both ways: every in-text citation has an entry in the list, and every entry is cited in the text, in one author–date style. A rewrite is more than a neater copy when it shows a deliberate change in my thinking, my evidence or my language, and I can say what I changed and why.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · homework or revision · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A conclusion should finish with one last piece of evidence that makes the judgement stronger. False — a conclusion adds no new evidence. Every claim was argued, with its citation, in a section.
  2. 2A conclusion may sharpen the degree of the thesis, but it must not contradict it. True — if the thesis said only in part, the conclusion cannot say entirely or not at all: the Resolve never contradicts the Judge.
  3. 3A conclusion that says ‘This report has looked at the town, the papyri and Herodotus’ is a strong one. False — it describes what the report did. A conclusion says what the report found, with its limits.
  4. 4A source that is in your reference list but is never cited in your text should stay there to show you read it. False — the list holds only the sources you cited. Delete the entry, or cite the source where you used it.
  5. 5Naming the evidence that could change your conclusion makes the conclusion weaker. False — it shows where your evidence stops, so the judgement is more honest, not weaker. It turns a limit into a test.
  6. 6If you copy your paragraph out neatly with the same moves, you have met your next step. False — a rewrite shows a deliberate change in thinking, evidence or language, and you can say what you changed and why.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · homework or revision · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. Explain what a concluding judgement must do. Answer the question at the thesis’s degree (it may sharpen, never contradict), draw the reasons together, state the limits, and add no new evidence, so no new citations.
  2. Explain how to name what could change a judgement. Turn each limit into a test: name the evidence that is missing and say which way it would move the judgement.
  3. Describe how to check a reference list. Check both ways: every in-text citation has an entry, and every entry is cited in the text; the name and year match; one author–date style, in alphabetical order.
  4. Why is a degree of extent not a hedge? A degree of extent (only in part) answers how far; a hedge (suggests, more probably) sets how sure a claim is. A concluding judgement keeps both.
  5. What does a rewrite show, if it is more than a neater copy? A deliberate change in thinking, evidence or language, and what I changed and why.
  6. What goes wrong if a conclusion adds a new source? The claim was never argued or cited in a section, so the reader cannot weigh it. New evidence belongs in a section, not in the conclusion.
