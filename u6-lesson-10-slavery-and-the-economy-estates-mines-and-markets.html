1 · The economy map · I do — where our sources place the work · 2½ minutes

Estates, mines, cities and a market: four settings

For each setting, who is telling us, and about when?

1 Owners writing farming handbooks: Cato (about 160 BCE), Varro (37 BCE) and Columella (mid-1st century CE). 2 Diodorus, a Greek historian writing between about 60 and 30 BCE, drawing on earlier writers. 3 Scheidel, a modern historian arguing from a model. 4 Strabo, a Greek geographer writing in the early 1st century CE. None of them writes from the point of view of an enslaved person.

  1. 1Estates (Italy)Owners’ handbooks list the workers an estate needs and give advice: Cato, Varro, Columella.
  2. 2Mines (Iberia)A Greek historian describes silver mines worked by ‘slaves’ and names no district: Diodorus.
  3. 3CitiesA modern historian says enslaved people were ‘disproportionately concentrated in the cities’: Scheidel (2011). Rome is marked as one example.
  4. 4A market (Delos)A Greek geographer says Delos could admit and send away ‘ten thousand slaves on the same day’: a claim about what the market could do, not a count: Strabo.

1 · The economy map · I do — think aloud · a speaker in a farming dialogue · 3 minutes

Varro 1.17.2–3: what does the advice reveal?

Source · a farming dialogue, on who does the work

All agriculture is carried on by men — slaves, or freemen, or both; by freemen, when they till the ground themselves, as many poor people do with the help of their families; or hired hands, when the heavier farm operations, such as the vintage and the haying, are carried on by the hiring of freemen; …

… this is my opinion: it is more profitable to work unwholesome lands with hired hands than with slaves; and even in wholesome places it is more profitable thus to carry out the heavier farm operations, such as storing the products of the vintage or harvest.

Varro, De Re Rustica 1.17.2–3, 37 BCE, a dialogue about farming that Varro addressed to his wife Fundania (the speaker is Scrofa; “this is my opinion” is his) · trans. W. D. Hooper and H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1934)

  1. 1Who speaksA speaker, Scrofa, in a dialogue Varro wrote about farming (Lesson 7).
  2. 2What he says is doneFarm work is done by ‘slaves, or freemen, or both’; hired hands do ‘the heavier farm operations’.
  3. 3His advice‘this is my opinion’: hired hands are ‘more profitable’ on unwholesome land and for heavy work.
  4. 4What to be careful ofIt is one speaker’s opinion. The extract does not say how many owners did what he advises.

What does an owner’s advice to hire free workers reveal?

That a farming writer, speaking as an owner, weighed profit when choosing between enslaved and free workers: he says hired hands are ‘more profitable’ on unwholesome land and for heavy work such as the vintage. It is his opinion, so it shows what he thought an owner should weigh, not how many owners did it.

2 · The evidence · We do — supported reading (1 of 2) · estates · 2 minutes · underline each job, circle what is not said

Estates: two model staff lists and a piece of advice

Source · a handbook, on the staff of two model farms

This is the proper equipment for an oliveyard of 240 iugera: An overseer, a housekeeper, 5 labourers, 3 teamsters, 1 muleteer, 1 swineherd, 1 shepherd — a total of 13 persons; …

This is the proper equipment for a vineyard of 100 iugera: An overseer, a housekeeper, 10 labourers, 1 teamster, 1 muleteer, 1 willow-worker, 1 swineherd — a total of 16 persons; …

Cato, De Agri Cultura 10.1 and 11.1, about 160 BCE, a handbook for farm owners (Cato, 234–149 BCE, was a Roman senator); model lists, not Cato’s own farm · 240 iugera is about 60 hectares, 100 iugera about 25 · trans. W. D. Hooper and H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1934)

Source · a handbook, on far distant estates

On far distant estates, however, which it is not easy for the owner to visit, it is better for every kind of land to be under free farmers than under slave overseers, but this is particularly true of grain land.

Columella, De Re Rustica 1.7.6, mid-1st century CE, a farming handbook for owners · trans. H. B. Ash (Loeb, 1941)

Count the workers in each of Cato’s lists. What do the lists say about them, and what do they leave out?

Thirteen (1 + 1 + 5 + 3 + 1 + 1 + 1) and sixteen (1 + 1 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1). Both lists give jobs, not legal status: neither says whether the workers were enslaved or free, and both are Cato’s model lists, not a record of a real farm. Columella advises owners of far distant estates to use free tenant farmers instead of ‘slave overseers’: his advice, for those estates.

2 · The evidence · We do — supported reading (2 of 2) · mines and a market · 1½ minutes · underline who gains, circle who bears the cost

A mine in Iberia and a market at Delos

Source · a Greek historian, on silver mines in Iberia

But to continue with the mines, the slaves who are engaged in the working of them produce for their masters revenues in sums defying belief, but they themselves wear out their bodies both by day and by night in the diggings under the earth, dying in large numbers because of the exceptional hardships they endure.

Diodorus of Sicily, Library of History 5.38.1, written between about 60 and 30 BCE, drawing on earlier writers; Iberia (Spain), no district named · trans. C. H. Oldfather (Loeb, 1939)

Source · a Greek geographer, on pirates and a market

The exportation of slaves induced them most of all to engage in their evil business, since it proved most profitable; for not only were they easily captured, but the market, which was large and rich in property, was not extremely far away, I mean Delos, which could both admit and send away ten thousand slaves on the same day; …

… The cause of this was the fact that the Romans, having become rich after the destruction of Carthage and Corinth, used many slaves; and the pirates, seeing the easy profit therein, bloomed forth in great numbers, themselves not only going in quest of booty but also trafficking in slaves.

Strabo, Geography 14.5.2 (Strabo was born about 64 BCE), early 1st century CE, on pirates from Cilicia (modern southern Turkey) · trans. H. L. Jones (Loeb, 1929)

What does each writer say the work or the trade produced, and for whom?

Diodorus: the miners produced ‘revenues in sums defying belief’ for their masters, while many died of the hardships; the sums are described, not counted, and he names only Iberia. Strabo: the profit from exporting captives drew the pirates in, and the Romans, who after growing rich ‘used many slaves’, are what he gives as the cause of the market’s size; ‘ten thousand’ in a day is a claim about what Delos could do, not a count.

2 · The evidence · We do — read the historians’ judgements · 3 minutes (read 2 · cold call 1) · circle each hedge, underline each claim

Two modern historians on enslaved labour in Italy

Source · a modern historian, on rural numbers and cities

… it would seem difficult to defend an estimate in excess of three quarters of a million for agricultural slaves, and a significantly lower total is certainly plausible. …

In the most general terms, there can be little doubt that, despite their potentially vital contribution to agricultural production, slaves were disproportionately concentrated in the cities.

W. Scheidel, “The Roman slave supply”, in K. Bradley and P. Cartledge (eds), The Cambridge World History of Slavery, vol. 1 (Cambridge University Press, 2011), p. 289. His estimate covers all of Italy’s farms, not only large estates. Scheidel says his estimate of “around 600,000 non-farming slaves” “cannot be more than a highly tenuous conjecture”.

Source · a modern historian, on the empire and the economy

But whether at the expense of expropriated peasants or alongside those who remained or both, the slave population engaged in agriculture increased substantially, its numbers fueled by the continuously successful wars of the period.

[The three revolts of enslaved people between 140 and 70 BCE] were therefore the direct outcome of the formation of Rome’s empire and the concomitant transformation of the Italian economy.

K. R. Bradley, Slavery and Rebellion in the Roman World, 140 B.C.–70 B.C. (Indiana University Press, 1989), p. 19.

What does each historian claim, and which words show how sure each is?

Scheidel argues that an estimate of more than three quarters of a million enslaved agricultural workers in Italy ‘would seem difficult to defend’, and that enslaved people were ‘disproportionately concentrated in the cities’; ‘would seem’ and ‘certainly plausible’ (of a lower total) are cautious, while ‘there can be little doubt’ is sure. Bradley argues that the enslaved population in agriculture ‘increased substantially’ and that the slave wars were the ‘direct outcome’ of the empire and the changed economy: a firm claim.

Cold call: how far did the economy depend on enslaved people?

A good oral answer starts with a degree word from the ladder, names a setting or a source, and gives one reason, in one breath. Several degrees can be defended: the test is how well each is supported, and whose words you rely on.

Worked example · written for this lesson · on a different question

Question: To what extent did the Spanish silver mines depend on enslaved labour?

Judge: The Spanish mines depended on enslaved labour to a large extent, because Diodorus describes enslaved miners producing “revenues in sums defying belief” (5.38.1).

Resolve that drifts: The mines therefore depended on enslaved labour only partly, although Diodorus names no district.

Resolve, fixed: The mines therefore depended on enslaved labour to a large extent, although Diodorus names no district.

Judge: ‘to a large extent’ = rung 2, largely

Drift: ‘only partly’ is two rungs down

Fixed: the same rung; the limit stays in the ‘although’ clause

We do · gesture vote

On Delos: To what extent did Roman demand explain the size of the slave market at Delos? Thumbs up = largely · sideways = partly · down = only to a limited extent.

  1. 1Roman demand largely explains the size of the Delos market, because Strabo gives as “the cause of this” that the Romans “used many slaves”.
  2. 2Roman demand partly explains the size of the Delos market, because Strabo also says exporting captives “proved most profitable” to the pirates.
  3. 3Roman demand explains the size of the Delos market only to a limited extent, because Strabo says the market was “not extremely far away”.

1 · up (largely). One rung down, to ‘to a significant extent’, if the pirates’ supply gets more weight.

2 · sideways (partly). One rung up or down: say which Strabo sentence you weigh more.

3 · down (only to a limited extent). One rung up, to ‘partly’: Strabo calls Roman demand “the cause of this”.

Practice and review · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1Cato’s list for an oliveyard of 240 iugera gives a total of thirteen persons. True — overseer, housekeeper, 5 labourers, 3 teamsters, muleteer, swineherd and shepherd; it is a model list (“the proper equipment”), not Cato’s own farm.
  2. 2Cato’s two lists say that the thirteen and the sixteen people were enslaved. False — they give jobs, not legal status; neither list says whether the workers were enslaved or free.
  3. 3Varro’s speaker says that all farm work was done by enslaved people. False — he says it is done by “slaves, or freemen, or both”, and advises hired hands for some work.
  4. 4Strabo says exactly ten thousand people were sold at Delos every day. False — he says Delos “could both admit and send away ten thousand slaves on the same day”: a claim about what the market could do, not a count.
  5. 5Scheidel’s “three quarters of a million” for “agricultural slaves” in Italy is a count. False — it is a hedged ceiling from a model (“it would seem difficult to defend an estimate in excess of” it), and he calls a related figure “a highly tenuous conjecture”.
  6. 6A Judge that says “largely” and a Resolve that says “partly” hold the same degree. False — they are two rungs apart on the ladder, so the paragraph ends on a different answer from the one it began with.

Practice and review · 2 of 4 · one degree

Fix a drift, then place degree words on the ladder

Exercise 1 · Fix this Resolve

Question: To what extent did Roman demand explain the size of the slave market at Delos?

Judge: “Roman demand largely explains the size of the Delos market, because Strabo gives as ‘the cause of this’ that the Romans ‘used many slaves’.”

Resolve: “Roman demand therefore explains the Delos market only to a limited extent.” Rewrite the Resolve so that its degree matches the Judge’s.

What a fixed Resolve needs · written for this lesson

The Resolve must sit on the same rung as the Judge (largely, not “only to a limited extent”). The limit can stay, in an ‘although’ clause, without changing the degree: for example, Roman demand therefore largely explains the size of the Delos market, although Strabo also gives the pirates’ profit as a reason.

Practice and review · 3 of 4 · extension

Do these two historians conflict?

Exercise 3 · a short paragraph

Bradley ties the slave wars to the transformation of Italy’s economy; Scheidel doubts that most enslaved people worked the land. Assess whether these views conflict.

Two historians · the extracts from the lesson

[The three revolts of enslaved people between 140 and 70 BCE] were therefore the direct outcome of the formation of Rome’s empire and the concomitant transformation of the Italian economy.

K. R. Bradley, Slavery and Rebellion in the Roman World, 140 B.C.–70 B.C. (Indiana University Press, 1989), p. 19.

In the most general terms, there can be little doubt that, despite their potentially vital contribution to agricultural production, slaves were disproportionately concentrated in the cities.

W. Scheidel, “The Roman slave supply”, in K. Bradley and P. Cartledge (eds), The Cambridge World History of Slavery, vol. 1 (Cambridge University Press, 2011), p. 289.

What a strong answer does

It says what each historian is actually claiming, and how sure each is (keep their hedges), before it says anything about conflict. It names a criterion for “conflict” (do the claims answer the same question?) and tests whether both claims can be true at once. It attributes each view in the present tense. It ends with a degree from the ladder, not a yes or no.

Practice and review · 4 of 4 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. How many workers does Cato list for an oliveyard of 240 iugera and for a vineyard of 100 iugera, and what do the lists not say? Thirteen and sixteen persons. They give jobs, not legal status, and they are model lists, not Cato’s own farm.
  2. What does Varro’s speaker advise about hired hands, and what kind of statement is it? Hired hands are ‘more profitable’ on unwholesome land and for heavy work such as the vintage. It is one speaker’s opinion in a dialogue (“this is my opinion”), not a record of what owners did.
  3. What does Columella advise for far distant estates? Free farmers (tenants) rather than ‘slave overseers’, which is ‘particularly true of grain land’. It is his advice, for those estates.
  4. What does Diodorus say the enslaved miners produced for their masters, and what does he not name? “Revenues in sums defying belief”. He names Iberia, not a district.
  5. What does Strabo say Delos could do, and what kind of statement is that? It could “admit and send away ten thousand slaves on the same day”: a claim about the market’s capacity, not a count.
  6. How does Scheidel describe his estimate of around 600,000 non-farming enslaved people in Italy? As no more than “a highly tenuous conjecture”.
  7. What must be true of the degree words in your Judge and your Resolve? They sit on the same rung of the ladder, even if the words change.
