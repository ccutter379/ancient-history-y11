Unit 5 · Historical investigation · Lesson 9

Claims from limited evidence: hedging and the synthesis paragraph

How sure can you be when the evidence has gaps, and how do you say so in a paragraph that joins several sources?

Year 11 Ancient History · Present with → or space · Read on a phone with R

Review · books closed · 6 minutes · press K for the timer

Five questions and a writing item

  1. On a research Cornell page, what goes in the cue column, and what goes beside every note? Your sub-questions go in the cue column; a citation goes beside every note.
  2. A magazine article reports what an excavator found. Does it corroborate the excavator? Why, or why not? No. It reports the excavator’s own evidence, so it is one witness heard twice: the two are dependent. Ask of each: how did the writer know? Weiss (2022), reporting Lehner’s dig, is an example.
  3. Name the five moves of a Stage 6 paragraph, in order. Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve.
  4. NESA defines examine as ‘Inquire into.’ What does an answer to an examine question do in this course? It looks closely at an interpretation: its claim, the evidence behind it and its assumptions.
  5. What is the difference between an interpretation and a representation? An interpretation is a historian’s evidence-based explanation of the past; a representation is a depiction of the past (a painting, a film, a reconstruction) shaped by its maker’s time and purpose.
  6. Writing · fix this citation: (Perseus, 2.124) (Herodotus 2.124). Perseus is where you read it, not who wrote it: the ancient author, book and chapter go in the brackets, and Perseus goes in the reference-list entry.

Key vocabulary · add to the unit word wall

Words for claims and paragraphs

Writing termsMeaning
Hedge (modality)A word or phrase that matches a claim’s certainty to the evidence: probably, suggests, on present evidence.
Synthesis paragraphA Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve paragraph that joins two or more cited sources.
Research termsMeaning
LimitationWhat a source, or the evidence as a whole, cannot show.
CorroborationSupport for a claim from an independent source.
Added todayMeaning
Degree of extentHow far a judgement goes: entirely, largely, partly, only to a limited extent. It answers how far; it is not a hedge.
Stacked hedgesTwo or more hedges on one claim, such as may possibly suggest. They commit to nothing. A hedge sets how sure a claim is: may (low), probably or suggests (medium), shows (high).
OverclaimA claim stronger than its evidence can carry, such as proves from one source.
Silence in the evidenceSomething the surviving sources never say, such as how the workers saw their own service.

1 · What the evidence can show · I do — each source, and its limit · then cold call

What can the evidence we have met show, and what can’t it?

SourceIt can showIt cannot show
Herodotus, Histories 2.124–125 · a Greek writer, about 430 BCE, passing on what Egyptians told him What Egyptians told a Greek visitor: the king’s orders, a people brought to “utter misery”, and gangs that served in turns: “They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months.” How the crews really lived. It is a claim passed on more than 2,000 years after Khufu, in a translator’s English.
AERA and Egypt’s Ministry · the excavators’ summary and a government page on the builders’ town A town “Purpose-built by the crown”, with bakeries, kitchens and royal grain silos; animal bones that the Ministry reads as the state keeping its workers in good health. Whether the work was willing, or what each worker received. The Ministry dates the town to Khafre’s and Menkaure’s workers, with an older settlement that “may date to Khufu’s reign”.
Merer’s papyri · an inspector’s logbooks, as Weiss (2022) reports Tallet’s reading In Khufu’s own reign, a phyle of about 40 men, one of four in its team, carried limestone by boat, and its bread and beer were fetched for it. The whole workforce. Tallet thinks the boatmen were “a very special category of workers”, and Weiss finds it “difficult to say” whether the quarriers and stone-lifters were treated as well.
Painted gang names in the relieving chambers above the king’s chamber · Lehner, NOVA Online (1997) Crews organised in named gangs, such as “the Friends of Khufu Gang”, which Lehner calls well-attested. What the gangs felt. Hawass says the name “proves” the work was not really pushed on them, but a name shows what a gang was called, not how its members felt.

Whose voice is missing from all four rows?

The workers’ own. Merer was an inspector, and his log records tasks and rations; the gang names are labels. On present evidence, no worker’s own account of how they saw their service has been found. So any claim about how it felt can only be hedged.

1 · What the evidence can show · I do — deconstruct a section question · then cold call

Deconstruct the question: verb · topic · limits · do

A section question on the class investigationHow far were the crews forced labour?

  1. 1Verb: how farA judgement of degree, like to what extent: not yes or no. It is a self-explanatory term, not a glossary key word.
  2. 2TopicHow far the crews’ work was forced: done because they were made to do it. Whether anyone owned them is a different question.
  3. 3LimitsThe sources we have met (Herodotus 2.124–125, AERA, the Ministry, Weiss 2022 and the NOVA interviews, 1997), so every answer is on present evidence.
  4. 4DoA five-move paragraph: a Judge with a degree (largely, partly) and one hedge (probably) · evidence from two or more cited sources · what it shows · what it cannot show · a refined Resolve.

Cold call: what does this question ask you to do?

Judge a degree: how far, not yes or no. Weigh more than one source, say what the evidence cannot show, and end on a judgement at the same degree.

2 · We do · joint construction — you supply each move, the teacher scribes · then cold call

Build one synthesis paragraph together: Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve

Cite as: (Herodotus 2.124) · (Lehner 1997) · (Ancient Egypt Research Associates n.d.) · (Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities (Egypt) n.d.) · (Weiss 2022)

MoveThe class decidesA model, scribed · written for this lesson
JudgeHow far? Give a degree (largely, partly), one hedge, and the gap. On present evidence, the crews’ work was largely forced, as a duty owed to the king, but it was more probably served in organised turns than by a people brought to “utter misery”, although how harsh that duty was cannot yet be shown.
EvidenceWhich sources, how do they relate, and how do you cite each? Herodotus reports that Egyptians told him Khufu made work compulsory, in turns of three months (Herodotus 2.124), whereas Lehner suggests that everybody owed a “labor tax” while conceding that it is not known whether the work was entirely coercive (Lehner 1997). The crown also supplied its crews: AERA describes royal silos issuing grain for bread and beer (Ancient Egypt Research Associates n.d.), and Weiss reports that an official fetched bread and beer for Merer’s team (Weiss 2022).
AnalyseWhat do they show together? Choose a precise verb. Together, silos and rations suggest a crown that planned its crews’ food, which fits an obligation organised in turns better than a population ground down by hardship.
EvaluateWhat can’t the evidence show? One limit for each source. However, the Ministry dates the town mainly to Khafre’s and Menkaure’s workers, Tallet thinks Merer’s boatmen were “a very special category of workers”, and Herodotus’s informants spoke more than 2,000 years after Khufu. Food does not show that the work was freely chosen, or how harsh it was.
ResolveRefine the judgement: the same degree, a sharper limit. On balance, then, the crews’ work was largely a forced but organised duty, more probably served in turns than in misery, although the evidence cannot separate the treatment of a special team like Merer’s from that of the many who quarried and hauled stone.

2 · We do · co-construct the class checklist · then cold call

What did that paragraph do that we should do every time?

Look back at the paragraph. Suggest one thing a strong report section does. Aim for six points, in your own words.

  1. Judge: answers the sub-question with a degree (largely, partly) and one hedge.
  2. Evidence: two or more sources, each named, attributed with an accurate verb and cited (author–date).
  3. Analyse: says what the sources show together, with a precise verb (suggests, indicates), never proves.
  4. Evaluate: names what the evidence cannot show (a gap, a later date, one team, sources that are not independent).
  5. Resolve: a refined judgement at the same degree, with no new evidence.
  6. Own words: paraphrase, not patchwriting; present tense for what sources say, past tense for what happened.

Worked example · written for this lesson, on the class question

On present evidence, the pyramid crews more probably served the king as an organised, fed duty than as a people brought to “utter misery”, although no worker’s own account survives to show how they saw their service.

Scope: what the claim rests on

Hedge: one medium word

Rival: named, so it can be tested

Gap: what the evidence cannot show

Frame: ‘On present evidence, … more probably …, although …’

Apply · in today’s task

Write the Judge of your own paragraph with the frame: a degree, one hedge and a gap clause. Your Writing toolkit (booklet pages 3–4): the modality scale.

We do · gesture vote

The Ministry reads the animal bones in the builders’ town as the state keeping its workers in good health. Vote on “3, 2, 1, show”: 1 finger = too weak · 2 = about right · 3 = too strong

  1. AThe bones prove the crews were well treated. 3 fingers · too strong. Prove is a high word: bones show what was found in one town, which the Ministry dates mostly to later kings, not how crews were treated.
  2. BThe bones suggest the crown fed its crews. 2 fingers · about right. One medium word, and a claim the evidence can carry: the Ministry reads the bones this way.
  3. CThe bones might perhaps hint at food. 1 finger · too weak. Three weak words stacked say almost nothing about evidence that can carry much more: the Ministry and Weiss both read the bones as the workers’ meat.

Then rewrite A or C so it is about right.

A: The bones suggest that the crews were well fed, although they cannot show how the crews were treated. C: The bones suggest that meat was eaten in the town.

The hedge you chose, and why

Finish the sentence about your own paragraph, then check yourself against today’s three “I can” statements.

“The hedge in my Judge is … because the evidence …, but it cannot show …”

A strong answer includes

the hedge itself (probably, suggests, may) and its level on the scale; the reason the evidence earns that level (how many sources, how independent, how close to the events); one thing the evidence cannot show; and, as a rule of thumb, no second hedge on the same claim (a degree of extent such as largely is separate).

A model summary · written for this lesson

A claim’s certainty should match its evidence, so I choose one hedge from the modality scale, such as probably or suggests, and never stack hedges or write proves from one source. I name what the evidence cannot show with a scope phrase such as on present evidence and a gap clause beginning although, because a precise gap tells the reader what would change my mind. A synthesis paragraph makes five moves: the Judge answers with a degree of extent and a hedge, the Evidence cites two or more sources, the Analyse says what they show together, the Evaluate names their limits, and the Resolve refines the judgement at the same degree.

Practice and review · homework or revision · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1‘The bones prove the crews were well treated’ is a safe claim, because the Ministry says the workers were in good health. False — prove is stronger than the bones can carry: they are food remains from one town, which the Ministry dates mostly to later kings, and the reading is the Ministry’s own. Suggest fits.
  2. 2‘May possibly suggest’ is a careful hedge. False — it stacks three hedges on one claim, so it commits to nothing. Choose one.
  3. 3Starting a claim with ‘on present evidence’ shows the writer is unsure of everything. False — it names what the claim rests on and that new evidence could change it. Other claims in the paragraph can still be definite (what a source says).
  4. 4A Resolve should repeat the Judge in the same words. False — it keeps the same degree but refines the judgement with the limits the Evaluate found.
  5. 5A strong synthesis paragraph names what the evidence cannot show. True — that is the Evaluate move, and the gap clause in the Judge. It tells the reader exactly how sure the writer is.
  6. 6If no worker’s own account of how they saw their service has been found, we can say nothing about the crews. False — we can still claim what the sources support (what Egyptians told Herodotus, a crown-built town, rations), hedged. The gap limits how sure we can be, not whether we may speak.

Practice and review · homework or revision · 2 of 4 · writing · the Assyrian practice question

Pitch it right: why did the Assyrian Empire collapse?

The evidence

Babylonian Chronicle (Livius.org 2020): “From the month Simanu until the month Âbu - for three months - they subjected the city to a heavy siege.”

Sinha et al. (2019), a climate record from a cave in northern Iraq: megadroughts “triggered a decline in Assyria's agrarian productivity and thus contributed to its eventual political and economic collapse”.

Exercise 1 · Too weak, about right or too strong?
  1. The chronicle proves that the siege alone ended the Assyrian Empire.
  2. The cave record suggests that drought contributed to Assyria’s collapse.
  3. Drought might perhaps have had some small effect on Assyria.

Say which is which, then rewrite one so it is about right.

The answers · written for this lesson

1 · too strong: one chronicle of one siege cannot prove that nothing else mattered, and Sinha et al. say drought contributed.

2 · about right: one medium word, and it is what the abstract says.

3 · too weak: stacked weak words, and “small” undercuts what the study reports.

A rewrite of 1: The chronicle records a three-month siege of Nineveh, which suggests that military defeat ended the city, but it cannot show why the empire was already weak.

A rewrite of 3: The cave record suggests that drought contributed to Assyria’s decline.

Label the five moves in a model paragraph

Exercise 2 · Label the five moves

Write J, E, A, Ev or R beside each numbered sentence of this model.

  1. 1On present evidence, the enemy alliance was more probably the main cause of Assyria’s fall than drought, although drought may have weakened the empire first.
  2. 2The Babylonian Chronicle records that the Medes and Babylonians besieged Nineveh for three months (Livius.org 2020), whereas Sinha et al. (2019) report from a cave climate record that megadroughts cut Assyria’s farm output and contributed to its collapse.
  3. 3Together, a siege and a run of droughts suggest an empire that was attacked while it was already weakened.
  4. 4However, neither source can show how much each cause mattered, and the chronicle is a Babylonian record, so it tells the story from the victors’ side.
  5. 5On balance, defeat more probably ended Nineveh, although a drought-weakened empire may have fallen more easily than a strong one.
The answers · written for this lesson

1 Judge: a degree (the main cause), one hedge (more probably) and a concession (although …); the gap itself comes in sentence 4.

2 Evidence: two sources, each cited, joined by whereas.

3 Analyse: what they show together, with suggest.

4 Evaluate: what the sources cannot show.

5 Resolve: the same degree, refined by the limit in sentence 4.

Practice and review · homework or revision · 4 of 4 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. How do you match a claim’s certainty to its evidence? Choose the level of the modality scale the evidence earns: low (may), medium (probably, suggests) or high (shows), and, as a rule of thumb, use one hedge per claim.
  2. Why is ‘may possibly suggest’ a weak hedge? It stacks hedges on one claim, so it commits to nothing. Choose one.
  3. What does a gap clause do? Give an example. It names what the evidence cannot show, so the reader knows how sure the writer is: although no worker’s own account survives to show how they saw their service.
  4. Name the five moves of a synthesis paragraph and what each does. Judge: a degree of extent (largely, partly) and one hedge. Evidence: two or more cited sources. Analyse: what they show together. Evaluate: what they cannot show. Resolve: a refined judgement at the same degree.
  5. Why must the Resolve not simply repeat the Judge? By the end the writer has weighed the limits, so the Resolve keeps the degree of extent but sharpens the judgement with them.
  6. What does ‘on present evidence’ tell the reader, and what does it not do? That the claim rests on the sources so far and could change with new evidence. It does not replace the hedge: the claim still needs one.
