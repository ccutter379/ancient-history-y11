VerbMeaning · NESA, Glossary of key words

1 · Writing focus · 8 minutes · I do, part 1 · the evidence · 1 minute

Writing focus: one body of evidence, two tallies of captives

Source · Strabo, Geography 7.7.3

… Polybius says that Paulus, after his subjection of Perseus and the Macedonians, destroyed seventy cities of the Epeirotes (most of which, he adds, belonged to the Molossi), and reduced to slavery one hundred and fifty thousand people.

Strabo, a Greek geographer (about 64 BCE – about 24 CE), credits this figure to Polybius. The sack of Epirus was in 167 BCE (Scheidel, 2011, p. 294). H. L. Jones’s translation (Loeb).

Source · Josephus, Jewish War 6.420

… the number of those that were carried captive during this whole war was collected to be ninety-seven thousand …

Josephus, a former commander of Jewish rebel forces in Galilee who later surrendered to, and allied himself with, the Roman commander Vespasian, wrote about 75 CE about the war that began in 66 CE and went on until about 73 CE. W. Whiston’s translation, 6.9.3.

Historian · Scheidel (2011), pp. 294–5

… while particular reports may well be exaggerated, they nevertheless cumulatively understate the actual scale of slave-making …

Walter Scheidel, “The Roman slave supply”, in The Cambridge World History of Slavery, vol. 1 (2011).

Ancient numbers are claims to weigh, not counts to add up. None of today’s four questions asks about this evidence, so you can watch one thing change: the thesis.

1 · Writing focus · I do, part 2 · 3 minutes · watch what the verb changes

Writing focus: the verb decides what the thesis does

I do · watch · written for this lesson · the bold words answer the verb
QuestionThe thesis must …A thesis, on the same evidence
To what extent do the tallies of Strabo and Josephus show how many people Rome’s wars enslaved?say how far, with a degree, then why

The tallies show how many people Rome’s wars enslaved only to a limited extent, because each is one report of a single campaign or war and, as Scheidel argues, such reports, taken together, understate the scale, although they do show that one war could enslave very large numbers.

Assess the scale of enslavement in the wars that Strabo and Josephus describe.judge how large, then weigh it

The enslavement in the wars Strabo and Josephus describe was probably very large, because they give 150,000 captives for one campaign and 97,000 for one war, although both are reported figures, not counts we can check.

Evaluate Josephus’s total as evidence for how many people the Jewish War enslaved.name the criteria, then judge against them

Judged by its date and its writer’s position, Josephus’s total is useful evidence of the scale of the enslavement, but weak evidence of an exact number, because he wrote about 75 CE, within a few years of the war, but as a former rebel commander who had joined the Romans.

NESA, Glossary of key words: evaluate — ‘Make a judgement based on criteria. Determine the value of.’ The three theses use the same two tallies and the same historian.

1 · Writing focus · We do → apply · 4 minutes · the “account for” version

Writing focus: now the class writes the “account for” version

We do · together · same evidence, a fourth verb

Account for the difference between the two tallies.

1 · Say it. With your partner, say a thesis aloud. Frame: “The two tallies differ mainly because …, whereas …”

2 · Vote. Which thesis does what account for asks?

  1. AThe two tallies differ only to a limited extent, because each is a reported figure, not a count, and both describe enslavement on a very large scale.
  2. BThe two tallies differ because Strabo gives 150,000 for Epirus and Josephus gives 97,000 for the Jewish War, so the first figure is larger than the second.
  3. CThe two tallies differ mainly because they count different things: Strabo reports Polybius’s figure for one operation, whereas Josephus gives one total for a whole war.
  4. DJudged by its date, Josephus’s tally is probably stronger than Strabo’s, because he wrote about 75 CE, whereas Strabo reports a figure that he credits to Polybius.
C: it states the reasons

Account for asks for reasons. A gives a degree (to what extent); B restates the numbers and gives no reason; D judges which tally is stronger (evaluate).

Apply · in your first plan, next

Start your judgement line in the verb’s own frame. Blank frames:

  • to what extent “… to a large / limited extent, because …”
  • assess “… was largely / only partly …, because …”
  • evaluate “Judged by [criterion], … is …, but …”
  • account for “… happened for [two] main reasons: …”

Whatever the verb

A strong thesis can be disagreed with; it answers the verb in the question’s own words; it names its reasons in order; its degree or verdict does not change between the thesis and the reasons. A thesis that retells the topic, or answers a different verb, is rewritten before anything else.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1An account for thesis states reasons; it does not need a degree. True — NESA: ‘Account for – state reasons for, report on.’ A degree answers to what extent.
  2. 2“To what extent” is one of NESA’s glossary key words. False — NESA lists it among the ‘self-explanatory terms’. Department advice: treat it as a judgement of degree.
  3. 3An evaluate thesis should name the criteria it judges against. True — NESA: ‘Make a judgement based on criteria. Determine the value of.’
  4. 4A thesis previews the argument by adding a sentence that says what the essay will do. False — it names its reasons, in the order the paragraphs make them, inside the thesis; our checklist bans a sentence announcing the essay.
  5. 5In a five-minute plan, the counter-view is the strongest case against your own judgement. True — it is the last step of the plan: deconstruct, judgement, three points, evidence, one counter-view.
  6. 6In HSC reading time you may underline or write notes on the exam paper. False — NESA’s 2027 HSC student guide says: “You must not write on or mark your exam in any way or use any equipment (including highlighters).”

Practice and review · 2 of 3 · writing · name the verb, then fix a thesis

Name the verb, then fix a thesis

Exercise 1 · which verb does each thesis answer?

Use the two tallies from today’s worked example. Name the verb: to what extent, assess, evaluate or account for.

  1. “Strabo’s figure for Epirus is reliable only to a limited extent, because it is Polybius’s figure at second hand.” To what extent: it gives a degree (only to a limited extent) and a reason.
  2. “Judged by its source, Strabo’s figure is weak evidence of an exact number, but useful evidence of a very large sack, because he credits it to Polybius.” Evaluate: it names a criterion (its source) and judges against it (useful for …, weak for …).
  3. “Josephus’s total is probably large, because it counts the captives of a whole war.” Assess: it judges how large, with a hedge and a reason.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What are the four steps of deconstructing a question? Verb, topic, limits, and what the answer must do.
  2. Name the five steps of the five-minute plan, and the minutes each takes. Deconstruct (1), judgement (1), three points with evidence (2), one counter-view (1); the evidence is gathered with the points.
  3. What does an account for thesis state? The reasons, in order, not a degree.
  4. What must an evaluate thesis name before it judges? Its criteria, such as the writer’s position, purpose, date and typicality.
  5. What does “previews the argument” mean, and what must a thesis not contain? It names your reasons in the order your paragraphs argue them; it must not contain a sentence announcing the essay.
  6. Is a degree word the same as a hedge? No. A degree says how far (largely, partly); a hedge says how sure (probably, may).
