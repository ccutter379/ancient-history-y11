Key vocabulary · the words for today’s writing

Words for a timed extended response

Writing termsMeaning
Assess‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’ (NESA, Glossary of key words) Department advice: make and support a judgement.
To what extentNot a glossary word: NESA lists it among the ‘self-explanatory terms’. A judgement of degree (largely, partly, only to a limited extent), held from the Judge to the Resolve.
Five-minute planA plan made on the clock: deconstruct the question, make a judgement, choose three points, find evidence for each and name one counter-view.
Time mapA plan for using time in a timed response: 5 plan · 6 introduction · 10 per body paragraph · 4 conclusion.
Topic sentenceThe first sentence of a body paragraph; it answers the question and picks up a reason from the thesis.
DegreeHow far something is true: entirely, largely, partly, only to a limited extent.
Also useful todayMeaning
PerspectiveA point of view shaped by a person’s position, purpose and audience.
InterpretationA historian’s evidence-based explanation of the past, which others may contest.
ConcessionAdmitting the strongest counter-view before resolving.
Resolving conclusionA conclusion that refines the thesis, adds no new evidence and ends on a judgement.
Change-overThe moment the time map says to start the next part of your response; your teacher calls it.

2 · Plan · 5 minutes · press K, click 5, then Start · on the first page of your writing booklet

Plan in five minutes

To what extent did Roman law and emperors limit owners’ power over enslaved people, c. 200 BCE – 200 CE?

What does this question ask you to do? Check your deconstruction.

Verb
to what extent: not a glossary word, because NESA lists it among the self-explanatory terms. Department advice: it asks for a judgement of degree, closest to assess (NESA, Glossary of key words: ‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’): how far, not yes or no
Topic
how far Roman law and emperors limited owners’ power over enslaved people
Limits
who limited (law and emperors) · what was limited (owners’ power over enslaved people) · c. 200 BCE – 200 CE
Do
a full extended response: an introduction (context, then a thesis with a degree that previews your three paragraphs) · three body paragraphs, each opening with a topic sentence linked to the thesis and running Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve · a conclusion that resolves in your thesis’s degree, with no new evidence · at least two perspectives, and one interpretation weighed
Planning is part of your writing time

Write your plan on the first page of your writing booklet. In the HSC, reading time is for thinking only. NESA’s 2027 HSC student guide says: “You must not write on or mark your exam in any way or use any equipment (including highlighters).”

Everyone · the five-minute plan
  1. Deconstruct · 1 minute: verb · topic · limits · do
  2. Judgement · 1 minute: your degree
  3. Three points · 2 minutes with step 4

Support

Plan template: a box for each step, frames allowed. The time map is on display. Topic-sentence frame: “Roman law and emperors limited owners’ power … [your degree], because …” Choose your own degree word. Your Writing toolkit (booklet pages 3–4): the modality scale and degree words.

Our class checklist · in short

Introduction: context · Thesis: a degree, previewing your arguments in order · Body paragraphs: topic sentence, the five moves, the degree held to the Resolve · Evidence: precise, more than one kind of source, a reference, every voice attributed · Language: modality matches the evidence, tense, links · Conclusion: same degree, why it matters, no new evidence, decisive

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1The five-minute plan is made on the clock, in your writing time. True — you write it on paper, as part of the time you have; in the HSC, reading time is only for thinking.
  2. 2When the change-over comes, finish the part you are on, even if the next part runs short. False — that takes minutes from a later part. Close the part you are on in a sentence, then change over (department advice).
  3. 3“To what extent” is one of NESA’s glossary key words. False — NESA lists it among the self-explanatory terms. Department advice: treat it as a judgement of degree.
  4. 4A conclusion may add one new, strong source if you have time. False — a resolving conclusion refines the thesis, draws the reasons together and ends on a judgement; it adds no new evidence.
  5. 5A topic sentence answers the question and picks up a reason from the thesis. True — it is the first sentence of a body paragraph, and it carries a degree.
  6. 6In HSC reading time you may underline or write notes on the exam paper. False — NESA’s 2027 HSC student guide says: “You must not write on or mark your exam in any way or use any equipment (including highlighters).”

A model · written for this lesson

The introduction took 12 minutes, twice the 6 the time map gives it, so body paragraph 3 lost 4 minutes and the conclusion lost 2. At the 6-minute change-over the student should have ended the introduction with the thesis in a sentence and started body paragraph 1 on time.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. Name the five steps of the five-minute plan, in order. Deconstruct, judgement, three points, evidence, one counter-view.
  2. What does the time map give each part of a 45-minute response? 5 minutes to plan, 6 for the introduction, 10 for each of three body paragraphs and 4 for the conclusion.
  3. What does “to what extent” ask for, and is it a NESA key word? A judgement of degree (largely, partly, only to a limited extent), held from the Judge to the Resolve, not yes or no. It is not a key word: NESA lists it among the self-explanatory terms. The “degree” advice is department advice.
  4. Name the five moves of a body paragraph, in order. Judge, Evidence, Analyse, Evaluate, Resolve.
  5. What must a conclusion do, and what must it not do? Refine the thesis in the same degree, draw the reasons together and end on a decisive judgement; add no new evidence, and do not just summarise.
  6. It is the change-over and you are mid-paragraph. What do you do, and why? Close the paragraph with a one-sentence Resolve and start the next part on time, because each later part needs its own minutes (department advice).
