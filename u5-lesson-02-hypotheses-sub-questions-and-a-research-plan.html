What makes a question investigable?

Everyone · one line

What makes a question investigable? Give one test, and say what it checks.

The three tests, and one rule

Arguable: two people could reasonably answer it differently, using the evidence. Focused: one place, one time, one issue. Answerable from sources: there are sources you can find and read. The rule: no overlap with a Year 12 Ancient History or History Extension option you will study.

Today: an investigable question needs a testable answer to start from, and a plan to test it.

How is a hypothesis different from a conclusion?

A hypothesis comes before the research, from background reading, and is provisional. A conclusion comes after, from all the evidence, and says how far the hypothesis held: confirmed, modified or rejected.

1 · From question to plan · I do — background reading on our class question, thinking aloud · then cold call

Background reading: what do I believe so far, and what would test it?

The class question · closed: no one may choose itHow far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?

Source · Herodotus, what Egyptians told him

… Kheops, who was the next king, brought the people to utter misery.

They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months.

Herodotus, Histories 2.124 · a Greek writer, about 430 BCE · the chapter begins They said · trans. A. D. Godley (Loeb, 1920), as revised by the Perseus Digital Library

Pictured: a Roman bust of Herodotus, 2nd century CE, probably not a likeness from life (The Met, 91.8).

Images: The Met (CC0 1.0). Full details on the last slide.

Source · the excavators’ overview of the builders’ town

Purpose-built by the crown, this city served as the base of operations for constructing the great pyramid complexes of kings Menkaure, Khafre, and probably Khufu.

… lined with craft workshops, industrial yards, bakeries, commissaries, kitchens, warehouses …

Ancient Egypt Research Associates (AERA), “The Lost City of the Pyramids (Heit el-Ghurab)”, aeraweb.org, no date · the team excavating the town since 1988 · an excavation-project overview

Where do the two sources point different ways, and what gap does that leave?

Herodotus’s informants describe hardship and huge gangs under a cruel king; the excavators describe a planned town the crown built and supplied for its workforce. The gap: AERA links the town to Menkaure, Khafre and only probably Khufu, so it may not show the Great Pyramid’s own builders. Research has to test that.

1 · From question to plan · I do — three sub-questions and a research plan · then cold call

Three sub-questions, each with its evidence and keywords

Sub-question · a section of the reportLikely kinds of evidenceKeywords
1 · What does Herodotus claim, and how could he know?An ancient text in translation: Herodotus 2.124–125; modern historians on Herodotus as a sourceHerodotus 2.124 · Kheops · Khufu · Godley
2 · What does the builders’ town show?A site and its finds: the excavated town at Heit el-Ghurab; the excavators’ reports and museum or ministry pagesHeit el-Ghurab · Lost City of the Pyramids · pyramid builders’ town
3 · How were the crews organised?Documents from Khufu’s own reign: Merer’s papyri from Wadi al-Jarf; the excavator’s publicationsWadi al-Jarf papyri · Merer · Tallet
Three starting sources · from the background reading
  • Herodotus, Histories 2.124, Godley’s translation as revised on Perseus
  • AERA, “The Lost City of the Pyramids”
  • Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, “Workers’ Town and Cemetery”

Test the plan: does each sub-question help answer the main question?

Yes. The first tests the claim itself and how reliable its teller is; the second and third test it against evidence from Khufu’s time or close to it. Together they let me say how far his account holds. A sub-question on how the blocks were lifted would not earn a place: it asks how the pyramid was built, not who built it.

2 · Practice: sub-questions · We do — background reading, then pairs write · then cold call

Practice: three sub-questions for the Assyrian question

The practice question · closed: no one may choose itWhy did the Assyrian Empire collapse in the late 7th century BCE?

Background reading · what an overview tells us
  • From northern Iraq, Assyria ruled what one study calls ‘the largest and most powerful empire of its time’.
  • After King Ashurbanipal died (631 BCE, in Livius’s dating), Babylon broke away under Nabopolassar, its king from 626 BCE.
  • A Babylonian chronicle records Babylonians and Medes together at Nineveh, Assyria’s capital, in 612 BCE: ‘they subjected the city to a heavy siege’. After 609 BCE, Assyria disappears from the sources.
  • Historians have stressed ‘internal politico-economic conflicts, territorial overextension, and military defeat’; a 2019 cave-climate study argues that drought ‘contributed to its eventual political and economic collapse’.

Livius.org, ABC 3 (after A. K. Grayson, 1975) · A. Sinha et al., Science Advances 5 (2019) · Pictured: the chronicle’s tablet, written about 550–400 BCE (British Museum, 1896,0409.6)

Images: Zunkir (CC BY-SA 4.0). Full details on the last slide.

3 · You do · Writing focus · 7 minutes · I do → We do → apply

Writing focus: propose an answer, pitched to the evidence

I do · watch

Propose: ‘Put forward (for example a point of view, idea, argument, suggestion) for consideration or action.’ (NESA, Glossary of key words) A hypothesis proposes an answer, with one hedge per claim, matched to the evidence so far.

Lowmay · might · could · possibly · it is possible that
Medium ✓probably · is likely to · suggests · indicates
Highshows · demonstrates · clearly · must

Worked example · written for this lesson, on the class question

The Great Pyramid was more probably built by organised crews working from a town the crown built for them than by a people driven to “utter misery”, since such a town has been excavated at Giza, although it may belong mainly to later kings.

Hedge: medium

Rival: than …

Reason: since …

Limit: although … may

Apply · in today’s task

At the top of your proposal, write your hypothesis with the frame “[X] was more probably … than …, since …, although …” Your Writing toolkit (booklet pages 3–4): the modality scale.

We do · hinge: A, B, C or D on “3, 2, 1, show”

Which hypothesis for the Assyrian practice question is pitched right?

  1. AThe chronicle proves that the enemy alliance, not drought, was the main cause of Assyria’s fall, since it records a joint siege of Nineveh.
  2. BThe enemy alliance, not drought, may possibly perhaps have been the main cause of Assyria’s fall, since a chronicle records a joint siege of Nineveh.
  3. CThe enemy alliance was more probably the main cause of Assyria’s fall than drought, since a chronicle records a joint siege of Nineveh.
  4. DWas the enemy alliance or drought the main cause of Assyria’s fall, given that a chronicle records a joint siege of Nineveh?
C: one medium hedge, a rival and a reason

A is too strong: one chronicle cannot prove a cause, before any research. B is too weak: stacked hedges commit to nothing testable. D only asks the question again: it proposes no answer. C commits to an answer research could confirm, modify or reject.

Check first: what makes a hypothesis testable, and how strongly should it be pitched?

Testable: it commits to an answer that evidence could confirm, modify or reject, and it names the rival. Pitch: the middle of the modality scale (more probably, is likely to), with a reason from your background reading, because you have not done the research yet.

While you draft · proposal conferences

Your teacher meets about half the class today and the rest next lesson, for about two minutes each. Bring your question and hypothesis: your teacher approves your question, or asks for one change.

What a pass looks like

Hypothesis: a definite answer, not the question restated; one hedge from the middle of the scale, not proves and not may possibly perhaps; a reason from background reading. Sub-questions: each covers a different part; together they let you answer the main question; a source could answer each one.

“My question is … My hypothesis is that … more probably … than …, since …”

A strong answer includes

a definite answer to your own question (not the question restated), one hedge from the middle of the scale, a rival the research could prove right instead, and a reason from your background reading. Research may confirm, modify or reject it: that is the point.

A model summary · written for this lesson

A hypothesis is a provisional, testable answer formed from background reading, so it is pitched in the middle of the modality scale, with a rival and a reason, and research may confirm, modify or reject it. Sub-questions break the main question into parts that become the report’s sections, and a research plan names the kinds of evidence and the keywords for each. For our class question, the plan tests Herodotus’s claim three ways: what he says and how he could know, what the builders’ town shows, and how Khufu’s crews were organised.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A hypothesis is the conclusion of your investigation. False — it is a provisional answer formed before the research; the conclusion comes after, and research may confirm, modify or reject the hypothesis.
  2. 2A strong hypothesis says the evidence proves its answer. False — before research you have only background reading, so a hypothesis is pitched in the middle of the modality scale: more probably, is likely to.
  3. 3Each sub-question should become a section of your report. True — sub-questions break the main question into parts, and each part becomes a section.
  4. 4Herodotus and AERA’s overview of the builders’ town describe the builders’ conditions in the same way. False — Herodotus’s informants describe a people brought to ‘utter misery’; the excavators describe a town the crown built and supplied for its workforce, though they link it to Khufu only probably. Both do describe huge, organised work for the king.
  5. 5If research makes you change your hypothesis, your investigation has failed. False — modifying or rejecting a hypothesis is a finding: say what evidence changed your mind.
  6. 6Good keywords come from your background reading. True — the overview gives names, places and terms to search with, such as Heit el-Ghurab or Wadi al-Jarf.

Practice and review · 2 of 3 · writing · use the frame

Pitch a hypothesis right; write a rival

Exercise 1 · Fix the pitch

A question on Unit 2’s evidence: How far was the burning of Persepolis a deliberate act? Say what is wrong with each hypothesis, then write one with the frame.

  1. “Arrian proves that Alexander burned Persepolis on purpose.”
  2. “The burning might possibly perhaps have been partly planned in some way.”
  3. “Was the burning of Persepolis deliberate, or did it begin at a drinking party?”
A model · written for this lesson

1 is too strong (one source; proves). 2 is too weak (stacked hedges). 3 restates the question.

The burning of Persepolis was more probably a deliberate act than a drunken accident, since Arrian, who drew on Ptolemy and Aristobulus, presents it as Alexander’s decision against Parmenio’s advice, although Plutarch and Diodorus tell of a drinking party.

Exercise 2 · A rival hypothesis

Write a rival to the class model hypothesis on the Great Pyramid, then name the evidence that would decide between the two.

A model · written for this lesson

The Great Pyramid was more probably built by a people the king drove to ‘utter misery’ than by organised crews working from a town the crown built for them, since Herodotus reports that Khufu ‘compelled all the Egyptians to work for him’, although he wrote more than 2,000 years later.

What would decide it: evidence securely dated to Khufu’s own reign about how the crews lived and worked, such as Merer’s papyri, and whether the builders’ town was already in use in his reign.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What is a hypothesis? A provisional, testable answer to your question; research may confirm, modify or reject it.
  2. Why read background before targeted research? An overview shows the main evidence, names, places and keywords, so you can form a hypothesis and choose sub-questions.
  3. What does a sub-question do? It answers one part of the main question, and it becomes a section of the report.
  4. What goes in a proposal? Question, hypothesis, three sub-questions, three starting sources, keywords.
  5. Name three kinds of evidence a research plan can list. Ancient texts in translation; objects and sites; modern historians and archaeologists (and scientists).
  6. How strongly should a hypothesis be pitched, and why? In the middle of the modality scale (more probably, is likely to), with a rival and a reason, because so far you have only background reading.
