Key vocabulary · add to the unit word wall

Words for defending a conclusion

Added todayMeaning
JustifyNESA: ‘Support an argument or conclusion.’ Department advice: state your conclusion and give the evidence and reasons for it.
Conditional languageLanguage for what would follow if something were true: ‘If … were found, I would …’
ContestabilityThe idea that interpretations are debated and can change with new evidence.
DirectionWhich way a find would move a judgement: towards a source’s picture, away from it, or neither (it only makes the judgement firmer or weaker).
Terms from earlier lessonsMeaning
Concluding judgementThe final answer, with its degree and its limits; no new evidence.
Degree of extentHow far a judgement goes: entirely, largely, partly, only to a limited extent. It answers how far; it is not a hedge.
Hedge (modality)A word or phrase that matches a claim’s certainty to the evidence: probably, suggests, on present evidence.
LimitationWhat a source, or the evidence as a whole, cannot show.
Further evidenceEvidence not yet found, or not yet used, that could make a judgement firmer or weaker.

1 · I do · think aloud on the class conclusion · what would move it back towards Herodotus?

What would move this conclusion back towards Herodotus?

How far does the evidence support Herodotus’s account of who built the Great Pyramid?

Conclusion

Herodotus’s account is therefore supported only in part. The evidence suggests that the crown organised and fed its crews, that their service was a duty owed to the king, and that the work was hard, but it does not show a people brought to “utter misery”. The judgement has limits: the town may belong mainly to later kings, Merer’s papyri describe one team, and on present evidence no worker has left a description of how the service felt, only an inspector’s work log.

The class model report’s conclusion (Lessons 10 and 13) · written for those lessons.

The conclusion rests on …A find that would move it back
Release. Herodotus reports gangs that served for three months at a time (Herodotus 2.124), and the papyri mention no work by Merer’s unit from January to March, which Tallet believes may have been a season when its workers could go home (Weiss 2022).A record of crews kept at the work all year, without release.
Food. AERA reports bakeries and kitchens at the town, the Ministry reports a large quantity of animal bones, and the papyri include accounts of regular food deliveries (Tallet and Marouard 2014).A record of crews kept without rations.
Reward. Merer’s team received gifts of textiles on several occasions, and Weiss (2022) reports that Tallet believes that at least some workers were “highly skilled and well rewarded for their labor”.A record that crews received nothing for their work.

One team: Merer’s papyri describe a unit of about 40 men, one of four such units (Weiss 2022, reporting Tallet’s reading). Merer’s log records what one team did, so one find about one team would not move the whole conclusion.

1 · I do · how the Lesson 2 hypothesis changed · then cold call

How the hypothesis changed

Hypothesis · Lesson 2 · before the research

The Great Pyramid was more probably built by organised crews working from a town the crown built for them than by a people driven to “utter misery”, since such a town has been excavated at Giza, although it may belong mainly to later kings.

Thesis · Lesson 10 · after the research

The excavated builders’ town, its food remains and Merer’s papyri support Herodotus only in part: they point to crews that the crown organised and fed, probably as a duty owed to the king, but not to a people brought to “utter misery”, although the town may belong mainly to later kings.

Stayed: organised crews

Stayed: the limit about later kings

Changed: why the crews served

Changed: a hedge became a degree of extent

Cold call: which source changed the hypothesis, and what did it change?

Herodotus himself, read beside Lehner. His gangs that served three months at a time (Herodotus 2.124), set against Lehner’s view that “it seems that everybody owed a labor tax” (Lehner 1997), turned organised crews into organised crews serving in turns, probably as a duty owed to the king (Lesson 7). Merer’s papyri fit it, but describe one team. The hypothesis was modified, not rejected. The rival picture changed wording too: Lesson 2’s “driven” is now “brought”, Herodotus’s verb in Godley’s translation.

1 · I do · answer a process question about your own research · 1½ minutes

The process answer: first idea, source, why, now

A process question on the class investigationHow did your research change your first idea about who built the Great Pyramid?

An answer · written for this lesson, on the class investigation

My first idea was that organised crews, working from a town the crown built for them, built the Great Pyramid. Herodotus’s gangs that served for three months at a time (Herodotus 2.124), read beside Lehner’s view that “it seems that everybody owed a labor tax” (Lehner 1997), changed it, because three months and then another gang sounds like service in turns. So I now say that the crews were organised and fed, probably as a duty owed to the king, although Merer’s papyri describe only one team.

My first idea: my hypothesis, in one sentence, as I wrote it before the research

The source that changed it: named, with author and date or book and chapter

Why: what the source showed me, with because

What I say now: a hedge and a limit (probably, although)

Your research log shows when your answer changed. Use its dates and entries.

2 · We do · cold call round · 3 minutes · then the class judges each find

Propose a find that would change the class conclusion

Cold call · one find each

What one find would most change the class conclusion, and which way would it move it?

Name a find that could really exist, say what it would show, and say which way it would move the judgement. Look back at the three limits: the town may belong mainly to later kings · Merer’s papyri describe one team · no worker has left a description of how the service felt.

Next: the class judges each find, and your teacher shows a verdict.

Judge each find on two things
  1. Direction: which way would it move the judgement: towards Herodotus’s picture, away from it, or neither (only firmer or weaker)?
  2. Degree of extent: would it change how far Herodotus is supported (only in part to largely, say), or only a limit or a hedge?

A hedge (probably, suggests) only sets how sure a claim is. A find can move a judgement and leave its degree of extent where it is.

Would it change the degree, or the direction?

A find that could be madeThe verdict
A firm date placing the town’s main buildings only in the reigns of Khafre and Menkaure. Direction: neither way: the judgement about Khufu’s crews becomes weaker. Degree of extent: unchanged. Merer’s papyri are from Khufu’s reign, so only in part stands, and one limit grows.
Records from other teams, such as the crews who quarried and lifted stone, showing the same food and organisation. Direction: away from utter misery, and firmer. Degree of extent: unchanged. It removes the one-team limit, but the part of Herodotus’s account that fits (gangs serving in turns) still stands, so only in part stays.
A worker’s own description of the service. Direction: either way, depending on what it says. Degree of extent: it could change. It is the only find here that bears directly on utter misery: a harsh account would move the judgement back towards Herodotus; a mild one would leave only in part where it is.
A list from Khufu’s reign of who owed work to the king, and for how many months. Direction: neither way on utter misery: it firms the part of Herodotus’s account the conclusion already grants, service owed to the king (probably towards shows). Degree of extent: unchanged.

Judge your own proposal the same way: which way would it move the judgement, and would it change the degree of extent, or only a hedge or a limit?

3 · Writing focus · speaking · 6 minutes · I do → We do → apply · on the class investigation

Writing focus: defend your conclusion and say what would change it

I do · watch

Justify: ‘Support an argument or conclusion.’ (NESA, Glossary of key words) Department advice: state your conclusion, then give the evidence and reasons for it. A conclusion you can defend also says what would test it.

‘If … were found, I would …; on present evidence, though, …’

Written for this lesson, on the class investigation

If a papyrus recorded crews held without release, rations or pay, I would have to move back towards Herodotus’s picture of a people brought to “utter misery”; on present evidence, though, Herodotus’s three-month gangs, the crews’ food and their organised units suggest that the crown organised and fed them, probably as a duty owed to the king, so Herodotus is supported only in part.

The find: something that could really exist, named exactly

The direction: which way it would move my judgement, said plainly

The turn: back to the evidence we have

The judgement kept: its reasons, one hedge for each claim, and its degree of extent (only in part)

3 · Writing focus · We do, then apply · hinge: A, B, C or D on “3, 2, 1, show”

Writing focus: concede a limit without collapsing

We do · which answer concedes without collapsing?

A listener asks ‘What would change your mind about how far Herodotus’s account is supported?’ You answer with this find: a firm date placing the town’s main buildings only in the reigns of Khafre and Menkaure.

  1. AIf it were found, I would abandon my conclusion altogether; on present evidence, though, I no longer have any judgement to defend.
  2. BIf it were found, I would hold my judgement about Khufu’s crews less firmly; on present evidence, though, Herodotus is supported only in part.
  3. CIf it were found, I would change nothing in my conclusion at all; on present evidence, though, Herodotus is supported only in part.
  4. DIf it were found, I would not know what to think; on present evidence, though, the evidence does not say, so I cannot judge.
B: a limit named, its effect said, its degree kept

A collapses: one limit is not a refutation. C concedes nothing: a firm later date would weaken the judgement about Khufu’s crews, and C denies it. D treats the evidence does not say as I do not know: the evidence says exactly what it cannot show, and a judgement on present evidence is still possible. B says what the find would do (make the judgement about Khufu’s crews less firm, moving it neither towards Herodotus nor away) and keeps only in part.

A model summary · written for this lesson

A defended conclusion is the best answer on present evidence, and it stays open to revision, so I can say what would change it without giving it up. Conditional language does this: it names a find that could really exist, says which way the find would move my judgement, and then returns to present evidence with my judgement and its degree of extent still stated. A process answer explains how my own answer changed: my first idea, the source that changed it, why it did, and what I say now, with a hedge and a limit.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · homework or revision · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A conclusion is the best answer on present evidence, and it stays open to revision. True — that is what contestability means: new evidence can change it.
  2. 2The strongest answer to What would change your mind? is ‘Nothing, because my evidence is solid’. False — it concedes no limit, so it cannot be tested. Name a find that could really exist, and which way it would move the judgement.
  3. 3‘If more evidence were found, I would think again’ is a strong conditional answer. False — it names no find and no direction. There may be more evidence is not a limit.
  4. 4The evidence does not say means the same as I do not know. False — it says exactly what the evidence cannot show, and a judgement on present evidence can still be made.
  5. 5A find that changes a limit must also change the degree of extent. False — a firm date for the town would weaken the judgement about Khufu’s crews, but only in part would stand, because utter misery would still not be shown.
  6. 6To justify a conclusion is to support it: state it, then give the evidence and reasons for it. True — NESA: ‘Support an argument or conclusion.’

Practice and review · 2 of 3 · homework or revision · speaking · say each answer aloud first

Two answers on the Assyrian question

Exercise 1 · A contestability answer

The question is the closed practice question, Why did the Assyrian Empire collapse in the late 7th century BCE? Your conclusion: on present evidence, the alliance of the Babylonians and Medes probably took Nineveh, although the chronicle cannot show how far drought had already weakened Assyria. You are asked What would change your mind? Answer with the frame ‘If … were found, I would …; on present evidence, though, …’

A model · written for this lesson

If a precisely dated record showed that drought had emptied Assyria’s farms before the siege, I would give drought more weight; on present evidence, though, the alliance probably took Nineveh.

Exercise 2 · A process answer

Suppose your first idea was that the alliance alone brought Assyria down, and then you read Sinha and colleagues (2019), whose cave record suggests decades of drought in the early to mid 7th century BCE, which they argue triggered a decline in Assyria’s farming. You are asked How did your research change your first idea? Answer in four moves.

A model · written for this lesson

My first idea was that the alliance of the Babylonians and Medes alone brought Assyria down. Sinha and colleagues (2019) changed it, because their cave record suggests decades of drought that they argue weakened Assyria’s farming. So I now say that the alliance probably took Nineveh, although drought probably weakened Assyria first.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · homework or revision · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. Explain what a defended conclusion is. The best answer on present evidence, open to revision. It can name what would change it and still be justified.
  2. Describe the parts of a conditional answer. A find that could really exist, named exactly; which way it would move the judgement; then, on present evidence, the judgement and its degree of extent still stated.
  3. Explain how the evidence does not say differs from I do not know. The first says exactly what the evidence cannot show, and still allows a judgement on present evidence. The second gives up.
  4. Outline the four moves of a process answer. My first idea; the source that changed it; why, with because; what I say now, with a hedge and a limit.
  5. What is the difference between a hedge and a degree of extent? A hedge sets how sure a claim is (probably, suggests). A degree of extent answers how far (only in part, largely).
  6. What does justify ask you to do? NESA: ‘Support an argument or conclusion.’ Department advice: state your conclusion and give the evidence and reasons for it.
