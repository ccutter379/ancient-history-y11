Review · writing item · 2 minutes · from Lesson 15 · cold call two

Does this conclusion resolve? Why or why not?

To what extent is Cato’s staff list for an olive estate (10.1) good evidence for who worked Roman farms? Written for this lesson: the question, the thesis and the conclusion are invented examples.

The thesis

Cato’s list is good evidence for who worked Roman farms only to a limited extent, because it is a model, it names jobs but no people, and it shows no child.

The conclusion

In conclusion, this response has looked at Cato’s list for an olive estate. It lists an overseer, a housekeeper and labourers. A speaker in Varro’s dialogue also says that free workers did farm work as well as enslaved people. Cato’s list is therefore a useful source and tells us a lot about Roman farms.

No: it does not resolve
  • Not the same degree. The thesis says ‘only to a limited extent’; ‘a useful source’ that ‘tells us a lot’ is a different degree, so the thesis is dropped, not refined.
  • Reasons not drawn together. It lists what the response did, not the three reasons (a model, jobs not people, no child).
  • No why it matters, and new evidence: Varro’s speaker appears here for the first time.
  • No decisive final judgement that fits: it ends on a judgement, but one that contradicts the thesis.

1 · Writing focus · 6 minutes · I do → We do → apply in your timed paragraph · part 1: run the check on a paragraph

Writing focus: run the three-minute check on a paragraph

To what extent does Columella’s advice to owners on managing enslaved people aim at profit? Not today’s question: watch the check, not the content.

I do · watch · a paragraph on Columella’s farm management · written for this lesson

Columella’s advice to owners on managing the enslaved people who work an estate aims largely at profit. Columella wrote his handbook for farm owners in the mid-1st century CE, and he warns that making an overseer of an idle enslaved man from a city household harms the whole estate, since ‘the master suffers not so much loss in the slave himself as in his whole estate’ (1.8.2); he also tells owners to have the overseer clothe the household for ‘usefulness rather than appearance’, so that ‘some work may be done in the open’ (1.8.9). These passages suggest that Columella values the enslaved people on an estate mainly for what they add to its output. However, a handbook records an owner’s advice, not what owners did, and Columella writes for owners, not for the people they enslaved, so his advice may show one owner’s priorities rather than a typical estate. Columella’s advice is therefore largely about profit, but it is evidence of an owner’s aims, not of how owners ran their estates.

The check: four questions

1 · Five moves: Judge · Evidence · Analyse · Evaluate · Resolve, in order. All five sentences are there; the Evaluate weighs the handbook

2 · One degree: the Judge sets it (largely) and the Resolve keeps it: the same rung, here in the same word

3 · Tense: past for events and acts (wrote, did, ran); present for what a writer does (warns, suggest, writes)

4 · Agents named: Columella, owners, the overseer: who acted is always in the sentence, not “it was advised” or “estates were run”

All four pass. The second paragraph, on Seneca, does not.

1 · Writing focus · part 2: We do → apply · pairs find two errors for 2 minutes, then we fix them

Writing focus: find two errors with the card

To what extent does Seneca’s Epistle 47 show concern for enslaved people themselves? Not today’s question: run the card, find two errors, say which check each fails.

We do · a second paragraph · written for this lesson · it has two errors

Seneca’s Epistle 47 shows concern for enslaved people themselves only to a limited extent. Writing to his friend Lucilius in about 62–65 CE, Seneca praises him for living ‘on friendly terms with your slaves’ (47.1) and urges owners to ‘Treat your inferiors as you would be treated by your betters’ (47.11). Yet enslaved people are silenced with the rod at a householder’s dinner (47.3). These lines suggest that Seneca’s kindness is aimed at an owner’s conduct, not at changing the lives of those he describes. Joshel argued that Seneca’s ‘pervasive use of slavery as a metaphor … drains social realities of meaning’ (2011, p. 234), so that ‘what counts is the mind and soul of the aristocrat, not the social reality of the slave’; however, Seneca also insists that ‘they are men’ (47.1), so the letter is not indifferent to them. Epistle 47 therefore shows concern for enslaved people themselves only to a limited extent: it asks for kindness, but mostly as a lesson in an owner’s character.

  • Cato 2.2: ‘slaves have run away’ is one of the excuses the overseer gives, as an owner’s handbook lists them: it shows what an owner expected, not a case of flight.
  • The sale edict (Gellius 4.2.1): the seller must say ‘which one is a runaway or a vagabond’: a rule for sale tickets, quoted by a later writer.
  • The First Sicilian war: it ended in 132 BCE (certain); its start is unknown, any year from 141 to 135 BCE. Both Tauromenium and Enna were taken by betrayal; ‘two hundred thousand’ is a round figure in a summary.
  • Spartacus: neither Plutarch nor Appian was alive in 73–71 BCE; the praetors’ names are unsettled; Plutarch has him ‘cut down’, Appian says ‘The body of Spartacus was not found’.
  • Bradley (1989): ‘a revolutionary hero of the historical class struggle’ is a view he reports others holding (p. xi); his own view is that the slave wars ‘were not revolutionary mass movements in any sense’ (p. 126).

Practice and review · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1‘Seneca said that owners should be kind’ is the right tense for what Seneca writes in a letter. False — what a writer does on the page is present tense: ‘Seneca urges owners to be kind’.
  2. 2‘The sick were abandoned on the island’ names who acted. False — it is an agentless passive. Name them: Suetonius says that certain men exposed their sick and worn-out enslaved people on the Island of Aesculapius.
  3. 3A paragraph whose Judge says ‘largely’ and whose Resolve says ‘partly’ holds one degree. False — they are different rungs: the Resolve keeps the Judge’s degree, even if the words change.
  4. 4In a three-minute check, start by correcting spelling. False — fix the highest-value error first: a missing move before a wrong tense, and spelling last (department advice).
  5. 5‘Claudius decreed that such people were free’ is right in the past tense. True — it reports what an emperor did, an event, so the past tense is right (Suetonius, Claudius 25.2); what Suetonius says about it is present tense.
  6. 6The Resolve may use different words from the Judge if it sits on the same degree. True — ‘largely’ and ‘to a large extent’ sit on the same rung; ‘largely’ and ‘partly’ do not.

What a strong answer does

It names one specific thing from the first judgement, not “everything”. It gives the evidence that changed it, with who made it and when, and says whose side that writer or historian writes from. It keeps a hedge, and says what the evidence cannot show. It passes the four checks: a degree, the tense for events and for writers, and named agents. There is no right thing to be wrong about: the answer is your own.

Practice and review · 4 of 4 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What are the four checks in the three-minute check, and which do you fix first? Five moves, one degree, tense, agents named. Fix the highest-value error first: a missing move before a wrong tense.
  2. Which tense for what happened, and which for what a writer or historian does? Past for what happened (‘Claudius decreed’); present for what a writer or historian does (‘Seneca urges’, ‘Joshel argues’).
  3. What is wrong with ‘The sick were abandoned on the island’? It is an agentless passive: it hides who acted. Name them (Suetonius says that certain men exposed their sick and worn-out enslaved people).
  4. What does a conclusion that resolves do? Refines the thesis in the same degree, draws the reasons together, says why the judgement matters, adds no new evidence, and ends on a decisive judgement.
  5. What is the difference between a degree and a hedge? A degree says how far a claim holds (largely, only to a limited extent); a hedge says how sure the evidence lets us be (perhaps, suggests).
  6. What does a reflection on your first judgement include? The evidence that changed your mind, with who made it and when; what changed and what did not; a hedge; and what the sources cannot show.
