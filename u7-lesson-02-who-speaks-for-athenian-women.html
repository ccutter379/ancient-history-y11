Two types of source, one shared limitation

Everyone · one line

Name two types of source about Greek women and one limitation they share.

Cold call: which two types did you choose, and what limitation do they share?

Two types · any two

Men’s texts (history, philosophy, drama, law-court speeches) · inscriptions and grave stelai · painted vases · papyri (nearly all from Egypt, where the dry desert preserved them) · queens’ coins and cult objects.

A limitation they share · any one

Almost all were made by men, and many for men · they show ideals more than daily life · they are mostly about elite women · few women’s own words survive.

Today: three sources from Classical Athens, read closely. Who made each one, for whom, and what is it useful for?

1 · A husband’s speech · I do — read the source, then the case behind it

Lysias 1: a husband speaks about his wife in court

Source · Lysias 1.7 · Euphiletus on his wife

It is true that in the early days, Athenians, she was the most excellent of wives; she was a clever, frugal housekeeper, and kept everything in the nicest order. But as soon as I lost my mother, her death became the cause of all my troubles.

Source · Lysias 1.9 · Euphiletus on his house

Now in the first place I must tell you, sirs … my dwelling is on two floors, the upper being equal in space to the lower, with the women’s quarters above and the men’s below. When the child was born to us, its mother suckled it; and in order that, each time that it had to be washed, she might avoid the risk of descending by the stairs, I used to live above, and the women below.

Lysias, On the Murder of Eratosthenes (speech 1), written for Euphiletus to deliver in his own defence, Athens, about 400 BCE (the date is uncertain) · trans. W. R. M. Lamb (Loeb, 1930), Perseus Digital Library

Words: frugal careful with money · suckled breastfed · Athenians, sirs: the men judging the case

  1. 1The caseEuphiletus, an Athenian, killed a man named Eratosthenes, who he said had seduced his wife. The dead man’s relatives prosecuted him for murder.
  2. 2Who wrote it?Lysias, a metic (a resident alien) who made “a regular profession of speech-writing for lawsuits”. He wrote the speech; Euphiletus spoke it, in his own name.
  3. 3To whom?A court of Athenian men: he calls them “Athenians” and “sirs”.
  4. 4Why?To be acquitted. The law he appealed to let a husband kill a seducer caught in the act, but not by a planned trap, so he had to show that he had not lured Eratosthenes to his house. His story begins with a husband who trusted his wife and for a long time suspected nothing.

1 · A husband’s speech · I do — think aloud: the OVPRL routine on page 2 of your booklet

Useful for what? Not for what?

OVPRLLysias 1 · thinking aloud
OriginA law-court speech by Lysias, a metic and professional speechwriter, for Euphiletus to deliver in his own defence; Athens, about 400 BCE (the date is uncertain).
ValueA husband’s picture of his household: a wife who was at first “a clever, frugal housekeeper”; a small house on two floors, with the women’s quarters upstairs.
PurposeTo be acquitted by a court of Athenian men: he must show that he trusted his wife and set no trap.
ReliabilityReliable for how a husband wanted his household to look to the court, and probably for how a small house could be arranged, since the jurors had to find it believable; not for his wife’s own experience.
LimitationsHer words reach us only through his defence; one household; only his side of the case.

Cold call: why does Euphiletus describe his house to the court at all?

It helps his defence. It explains how the affair could go on in his own house while he slept upstairs: his wife “would often leave me and go down to sleep with the child” (1.10). A husband who arranged his house this way, he implies, for a long time suspected nothing.

2 · A dialogue and a vase · We do — supported reading: read it together, then answer in pairs

A husband describes his young bride

Source · Xenophon, Oeconomicus 7.5–7.6 · Ischomachus speaks to Socrates

Why, what knowledge could she have had, Socrates, when I took her for my wife? She was not yet fifteen years old when she came to me, and up to that time she had lived in leading-strings, seeing, hearing and saying as little as possible. [7.6] If when she came she knew no more than how, when given wool, to turn out a cloak, and had seen only how the spinning is given out to the maids, is not that as much as could be expected?

Xenophon (about 430–354 BCE), a wealthy Athenian and friend of Socrates, Oeconomicus (a dialogue on managing an estate), probably written after 362 BCE: Socrates reports a conversation with Ischomachus, an Athenian gentleman with an estate · trans. E. C. Marchant (Loeb, 1923), Perseus Digital Library

Words: leading-strings straps that support a small child learning to walk; here, kept under close control · turn out a cloak make a cloak from the wool · maids women servants in the house

  1. 1Who speaks, and who reports it?Ischomachus speaks; Socrates reports the conversation; Xenophon wrote the whole dialogue.
  2. 2How old was she when she married?“Not yet fifteen”; the extract does not give his age.
  3. 3How had she been brought up?Kept under close control, “seeing, hearing and saying as little as possible”.
  4. 4What did she know, and what does that suggest about the household?Wool-work, and how spinning is given out to the maids: a household with servants, so probably a wealthy one.

2 · A dialogue and a vase · We do — supported looking: look first, then read what the museum says

A painted box: women at home?

The museum’s description · Art Institute of Chicago

Nine women are depicted in the figured scene that runs right around the vase. The women are presumably in the gynaikeion or women’s quarters, and include a seated woman with a book roll, a woman holding wreath, a woman with a mirror … a woman with a lyre … a girl standing stiff and straight to the right, who might be the bride-to-be … Vessels of this type were probably used to hold personal effects like cosmetics, jewelry, or other trinkets and were often given as wedding gifts.

Pyxis (container for personal objects), terracotta, red-figure, Athens, 430–420 BCE; the museum names no potter or painter · description © Art Institute of Chicago, CC BY 4.0

  1. 1What do you see on this side?Three women in long robes; the one on the left holds a lyre. The museum says the scene runs right round the box.
  2. 2Who made it, and probably for whom?An unnamed potter and painter in Athens; probably for a woman’s personal things, and such boxes were often wedding gifts.
  3. 3Where are the women, according to the museum, and how sure is it?“Presumably” in the women’s quarters: a careful guess, not a certainty.

Cold call: what can the pyxis show that Xenophon cannot, and the other way round?

The pyxis, probably made for a woman’s use, shows women together with their own things, but gives no names and no words. Xenophon gives details a picture cannot, such as a bride’s age, upbringing and tasks, but only in a husband’s words. Both show ideals, probably of well-off households, and in neither does a woman speak for herself.

Writing focus: the usefulness question in short form

I do · watch

Four moves: provenance phrase → value for the question → limitation → judgement. 5 marks: a developed paragraph (department advice).

How useful is Lysias 1 for understanding the lives of women in Classical Athens? (5 marks) Worked example · written for this lesson

Written by the speechwriter Lysias for a husband on trial for killing his wife’s lover, Lysias 1 was spoken in his defence to a court of Athenian men. It is useful for how a husband described his wife’s role, since Euphiletus praises her as at first ‘a clever, frugal housekeeper’, and for the layout of a small house ‘with the women’s quarters above and the men’s below’. However, every detail serves his defence, persuading the jurors that he trusted his wife and set no trap, so the speech shows her as he needed them to see her. Lysias 1 is therefore reliable for how an Athenian husband wanted his household to appear to a jury in about 400 BCE, but not for his wife’s experience, because her words reach us only through his defence.

3 · You do · after you write · compare yours with a model

A model usefulness answer: four moves, one judgement

5 marks · a model written for this lesson, to read after you write

Written by Xenophon, a wealthy Athenian and friend of Socrates, probably after 362 BCE, the Oeconomicus is a dialogue on running an estate, in which Socrates reports Ischomachus’s account of how he trained his wife. It is useful for an ideal of a young wife’s life in a wealthy household: she came to him ‘not yet fifteen’, raised ‘seeing, hearing and saying as little as possible’, and knowing only wool-work and how spinning was given out to the maids. However, these are a husband’s words about his own wife, shaped to teach estate management, and she does not speak in this passage. The Oeconomicus is therefore reliable for the ideal of a young wife that a wealthy Athenian man could hold up in the 4th century BCE, but not for the lives of most Athenian women, because it describes one wealthy household through a husband’s words.

Practice and review · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1Lysias delivered the speech On the Murder of Eratosthenes himself. False — Lysias, a professional speechwriter, wrote it for Euphiletus, the defendant, who spoke it in his own defence.
  2. 2In Lysias 1.9, Euphiletus says the women’s quarters were upstairs. True — “the women’s quarters above and the men’s below”; after the baby was born he lived above and the women below.
  3. 3Ischomachus’s wife was about twenty when she married. False — he says she was “not yet fifteen” (Oeconomicus 7.5).
  4. 4The Oeconomicus gives the wife’s own account of her training. False — Ischomachus speaks, as Socrates reports him, in a dialogue Xenophon wrote; she does not speak in 7.5–7.6.
  5. 5The Chicago pyxis was probably used to hold personal things. True — the museum: such vessels were “probably used to hold personal effects” and were often wedding gifts.

Exercise 2 · 5 marks

How useful is the Chicago pyxis for understanding whether women in Classical Athens could read? The seated woman with the book roll is on the far side of the pyxis: use the museum’s description.

A model · written for this lesson

Made in Athens in about 430–420 BCE, the Chicago pyxis is a painted box probably used for a woman’s personal things. It is useful because, among the nine women painted around it, the museum describes ‘a seated woman with a book roll’, which suggests that a woman with a book roll could be pictured as part of an ideal household. However, the scene is idealised and the women are unnamed, so one painted roll cannot show whether any real woman could read it, or how many could. The pyxis is therefore reliable for how one Athenian painter could imagine a woman with a book, but not for how widely women could read, because it is a single idealised scene.

Practice and review · 3 of 4 · extension · assess

How does a defence shape a portrait of a wife?

Exercise 3 · one paragraph

Lysias 1 is a defence speech: assess how Euphiletus’s need to persuade a jury shapes his portrait of his wife, and what it is still reliable for.

Shape: Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve, with the same degree in the Judge and the Resolve.

Source · Lysias 1.6 and 1.10 · Euphiletus

… I kept a watch on her as far as possible … But when a child was born to me, thence-forward I began to trust her, and placed all my affairs in her hands … [1.10] … I never suspected, but was simple-minded enough to suppose that my own was the chastest wife in the city.

trans. W. R. M. Lamb (Loeb, 1930), Perseus Digital Library

A model paragraph · written for this lesson

Euphiletus’s need to persuade the jury shapes his portrait of his wife to a large extent, although the speech is still useful for the ordinary details of his household. The law he appealed to, as his translator W. R. M. Lamb summarises it, let a husband kill a seducer ‘taken in the act suddenly and not by premeditation’, so the defence had to show that he set no trap. His wife is drawn to fit that need: watched at first, then trusted once their child was born, when he ‘placed all my affairs in her hands’, and praised as having been ‘in the early days … the most excellent of wives’, so that he appears too trusting to have planned anything. Yet details the jurors had to find believable, such as a small house with the women’s quarters upstairs, are probably reliable, because a defence built on an unlikely household would weaken itself. On balance, the portrait of his wife is, to a large extent, his defence rather than her character, but the setting of an Athenian household is still worth using.
