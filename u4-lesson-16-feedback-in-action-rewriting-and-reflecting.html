1 · Whole-class feedback · 2½ minutes · the most common next steps · before → after

Next steps 1–3: the introduction, the thesis, the body paragraphs

Every Before is an invented sentence, written for this lesson, on a parallel question: Assess the role of Suetonius Paulinus in the revolt of 60 or 61 CE.

1Set the contextwho, when, where, in expanded noun groups; limit the key term

BeforeSuetonius Paulinus was a Roman. He was in charge of Britain. Then there was a revolt.

AfterIn 60 or 61 CE, when the Iceni and the Trinovantes rose against Roman rule in eastern Britain, Suetonius Paulinus, the governor of the province, was far away, campaigning on the island of Mona; his role here means the decisions he made as governor and general, from the outbreak to the final battle.

Why it works: who, when and where in expanded noun groups (the governor of the province, the island of Mona), then one limit on the key word, role, before the thesis.

2Thesis: answer ‘assess’a judgement of degree that previews the reasons; no sentence announcing the essay

BeforeThis essay will discuss the role of Suetonius Paulinus in the revolt of 60 or 61 CE, which was very important.

AfterSuetonius Paulinus played only a small part in the outbreak of the revolt but a decisive part in its defeat: his campaign on Mona gave the rebels their chance but did not cause the rising, while his decision to give up Londinium and his victory in the final battle saved Roman rule in Britain.

Why it works: NESA’s assess asks for ‘a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size’: how much? The after gives a degree and previews three reasons, in order, and announces nothing.

3Body paragraphs: judge, don’t narratea judgement sentence linked to the thesis, then the five moves

BeforeSuetonius marched to Londinium. Then he left it. Then the rebels destroyed Londinium and Verulamium. Then he fought the battle.

AfterGiving up Londinium was the first reason Suetonius’s part in the revolt’s defeat was decisive. In Tacitus’s account he resolved ‘to save the province at the cost of a single town’ (Annals 14.33), which suggests a general who put his small army before the town. … On balance, the decision cost Rome a town but kept its army in the field.

Why it works: it opens with a judgement linked to the thesis, says what the evidence shows (suggests), not what happened next, and, after the Evaluate (…), resolves in the thesis’s degree.

1 · Whole-class feedback · 2½ minutes · the most common next steps · before → after

Next steps 4–6: evidence, language, the conclusion

Every Before is an invented sentence, written for this lesson, on the same parallel question: the role of Suetonius Paulinus.

4Evidence: give it provenancea signal phrase, who wrote it and when, a short quotation, a reference

Before‘Britain would have been lost’ if Suetonius had not come back.

AfterTacitus, writing in 98 CE about the general on whose staff his father-in-law had served, claims that without Suetonius’s ‘prompt succour, Britain would have been lost’ (Agricola 16).

Why it works: a signal phrase, the writer’s date and position, and a reference: now the reader can weigh the claim.

5Language: match the modalityas sure as the evidence allows; link each sentence to the last

BeforeTacitus shows that Suetonius chose the perfect battlefield, so the victory was all his doing.

AfterYet only Tacitus says Suetonius chose his ground (Annals 14.34); Dio says he was ‘compelled, contrary to his judgment, to engage them’ (62.8), so the victory may owe as much to necessity and to his soldiers’ discipline as to his choice.

Why it works: shows and all his doing claim more than one source can carry; only Tacitus says and may fit the evidence, and Yet links the sentence to the one before.

6Conclude with a refined judgementthe thesis in the same degree, the reasons drawn together, a decisive end

BeforeIn conclusion, this essay has looked at Mona, Londinium and the final battle, and Suetonius was important in all of them.

AfterUltimately, Suetonius mattered less for how the revolt began than for how it ended: his absence on Mona let it break out, but his choices at Londinium and in the final battle decided it.

Why it works: it sharpens the thesis (less … than), draws the reasons together and adds no new evidence.

An annotated model: the introduction and the first body paragraph

Assess the role of Boudicca in the revolt of 60 or 61 CE. (The numbers are our checklist points.)

IntroductionIn 60 or 61 CE, while the governor Suetonius Paulinus was campaigning on the island of Mona, the Iceni of eastern Britain and their neighbours the Trinovantes rose against Roman rule, destroying Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium before they were defeated in a final battle. Boudicca, the widow of the Iceni’s king Prasutagus, played a decisive role in the outbreak of the revolt but a smaller one in its spread and its outcome: the wrongs done to her family and her royal standing explain why the Iceni rose, but the grievances of other Britons explain why so many joined them, and Suetonius and his army explain why they lost.2

Body paragraph 1The strongest case for Boudicca’s importance is that the wrongs done to her family gave the Iceni a cause, and both Roman writers make her its leader.3 Tacitus, writing about 55 years later, records that Prasutagus’s kingdom ‘was plundered by centurions, his house by slaves’ and that Boudicca was flogged (Annals 14.31); in the Agricola (98 CE) the Britons rose ‘under the leadership of Boudicea, a woman of kingly descent’ (16). Cassius Dio, writing about 150 years after the revolt, says she ‘directed the conduct of the entire war’ (62.2).4 This suggests that her treatment turned an Iceni grievance into armed revolt and that her royal standing gave it a leader. Yet both writers had reasons to stress her: Tacitus, who blames Rome’s own officials (14.31–32), gives her a real cause, and Dio says defeat by a woman caused Rome ‘the greatest shame’ (62.1); the Annals, unlike Dio, name no leader for the attacks on the towns (14.32–33).3 On balance, her role was decisive in starting the revolt, even if the sources cannot show how far she directed what followed.3

2 · Feedback in action · after your rewrite If time · or at home

The model, continued: two more paragraphs and the conclusion

Body paragraph 2Her leadership, however, cannot by itself explain why the revolt spread beyond the Iceni.3 Tacitus says the Iceni ‘stirred to revolt the Trinobantes and others’, and that the rebels’ hatred was ‘most intense’ against the veterans of Camulodunum, who ‘drove people out of their houses’ (Annals 14.31); Dio’s first reason is money: the procurator Decianus Catus maintained that Claudius’s grants ‘were to be paid back’ (62.2). These grievances belonged to many Britons, not to Boudicca alone, which suggests that she led a revolt that others already had reasons to join. Dio does make her ‘chiefly instrumental in rousing the natives’ (62.2), but even he names Rome’s money demands first.3 Her role in the revolt’s spread was therefore real but secondary to the grievances of the peoples who followed her.

Body paragraph 3Her part in the revolt’s outcome was smaller still, because both writers make its defeat a Roman victory, and Tacitus credits it to Suetonius Paulinus. Tacitus has Boudicca rally ‘tribe after tribe’ from a chariot (Annals 14.35), and Dio says she ‘assigned the others to their several stations’ (62.8); yet in Tacitus it is Suetonius who decides the war, giving up Londinium ‘to save the province at the cost of a single town’ (14.33) and choosing a defile ‘closed in at the rear by a forest’ (14.34). This suggests that, in Tacitus’s account at least, the outcome turned on Roman decisions more than on anything she did. However, only Tacitus, whose father-in-law had served on Suetonius’s staff, says the governor chose his ground; in Dio, as he ‘grew short of food and the barbarians pressed relentlessly upon him’, he was ‘compelled, contrary to his judgment, to engage them’ (62.8), which may mean that hunger and the Britons’ pressure, not Suetonius, decided when the battle was fought.3 Her role in the defeat was therefore limited, though perhaps not negligible: her army’s pressure may have helped to force the battle, but both writers make the result Rome’s.3

ConclusionUltimately, Boudicca’s importance lay in beginning the revolt rather than in spreading or deciding it. The wrongs done to her family and her royal standing explain why the Iceni rose and who led them, but the grievances of other Britons explain why so many followed, and Suetonius and his army explain why they lost. Because every account of her was written by Romans between about 37 and 150 years later, the Boudicca of the sources is partly shaped by Roman ideas; even so, the evidence supports her as the revolt’s leader, not as its only cause.6

Practice and review · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1In the Annals, Tacitus names Boudicca as the leader of the attacks on Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium. False — the Annals name no leader there, only “the barbarians” and “the enemy” (14.32–33); the Agricola puts the attack on the colony under her leadership (16), and Dio credits her.
  2. 2Dio says Buduica directed the conduct of the entire war. True — Dio 62.2; but he wrote about 150 years after the revolt, and Book 62 survives mainly in a later summary.
  3. 3Tacitus and Dio agree that Suetonius chose when and where to fight the final battle. False — in Tacitus he chose a position (Annals 14.34); in Dio he was “compelled, contrary to his judgment, to engage them” (62.8).
  4. 4A resolving sentence is a good place to add one last piece of evidence. False — it weighs the evidence the paragraph has already given and links back to the thesis, in the same degree; new evidence needs its own analysis.
  5. 5When you discuss what his text says, “Tacitus records” is better than “Tacitus recorded”. True — present tense for what a source or historian says; past tense for the events.
  6. 6“Londinium was abandoned” tells the reader who abandoned it. False — the passive hides the agent; “Suetonius abandoned Londinium” names him (Annals 14.33).

A strong rewrite includes
  • precise attribution in the present tense: “Richard Hingley suggests …”, and his own hedge (may, difficult really to know);
  • a distinction between the woman (what Tacitus, Dio and the finds can show) and the legend (what later ages made of her), the order Hingley describes for his book with Unwin;
  • a weighed response, not agreement by default: if much of the province did not rebel, what does that do to her role in the revolt’s spread?
  • a judgement that is refined, not abandoned, and still ends the conclusion;
  • no new evidence beyond the historian’s view itself.

Quick checks

  1. What does NESA say assess means, and what must a thesis for an ‘assess’ question do? ‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’ The thesis makes a judgement of degree (how much?) and previews its reasons, in order.
  2. What is provenance, and why does it belong in the sentence with the evidence? Who wrote or found the evidence, when, and from what position; the reader needs it to weigh the evidence (Tacitus about 55 years later; Dio about 150).
  3. How do you turn a narrative paragraph into an analytical one? Open with a judgement sentence linked to the thesis, and after each piece of evidence say what it shows (suggests, reveals), instead of what happened next.
  4. What does a strong Evaluate move do? It gives the rival cause or rival account its best evidence, then judges it with modality, instead of dismissing it as biased.
  5. What should a conclusion do, and not do? Refine the thesis, draw the reasons together and end on the judgement; not summarise what the essay did, and add no new evidence.
  6. Name the four sentence checks. Combine (because, but, so) · rename (an appositive) · name the agent · fix the tense (past for events; present for what a source or historian says).
