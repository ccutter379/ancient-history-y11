A strong line

A claim heading states the section’s answer, so a source could support or challenge it, and a term is named for its exact meaning. From Lesson 11’s class model: ‘Fed at the crown’s expense’ is a claim heading; ‘The papyri’ only names a topic. Provenance means where a piece of evidence comes from: who wrote or found it, and when.

1 · Writing from notes · Writing focus · We do (3 minutes) · a hinge · vote on “3, 2, 1, show” · then apply

Which sentence was written from the note, with the source closed?

The source sentence · class question, closed

They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months.

Herodotus, Histories 2.124 · trans. A. D. Godley (Loeb, 1920), as revised by the Perseus Digital Library

The student’s note · written for this lesson
100,000 in a crew · three months each · what Egyptians told him (Herodotus 2.124)
Apply · in your own draft

Write your section’s topic sentence from your notes, with your sources closed: it is the first thing you do in the drafting. Then check it against the original, and cite it. Your Writing toolkit (booklet pages 3–4): attribution verbs.

  1. AHerodotus reports that they laboured in crews of a hundred thousand men, each crew for three months (Herodotus 2.124).
  2. BHerodotus reports that they worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months (Herodotus 2.124).
  3. CHerodotus reports that every stint of work lasted three months, and that each crew numbered 100,000 men (Herodotus 2.124).
  4. DHerodotus reports that every stint of work lasted three months, and that each crew numbered 100,000 men, as Egyptians told him.
C: the one that changes the structure and cites

C changes the source’s structure: the length of the stint comes first and the size of the crew second, in the note’s words (crew, 100,000), and the citation gives the book and chapter. A is patchwriting: laboured and crews swapped into the source’s sentence, in its order, even with a citation. B copies the source’s words with no quotation marks: a citation does not make copied words yours. D changes the structure but has no citation: as Egyptians told him says who said it, not where a reader can find it.

Cold call: what makes C different from A and B?

2 · Planning · 6 minutes · the conditions · check first · then choose your section

Planning: what the drafting needs from you

Conditions
  • Individual and quiet.
  • Allowed: your notes, research log, synthesis grid and annotated source list.
  • Not allowed: draft text you prepared earlier. Today’s draft is written today.
  • Paper or a device, and whether the internet is on, are your teacher’s choice. Your teacher will say which.
  • Your draft is collected, and comes back next lesson with one strength and one next step.
Everyone
  1. Choose the sub-question your section answers.
  2. Check your claim heading and topic sentence. Fix one thing.
  3. List the source and evidence for each move (next).

Check first: what does your sub-question ask you to do?

Verb: how far, why or how: it sets the structure (a degree, or causes ranked). Topic: what you are writing about. Limits: the sources you have, so on present evidence. Do: a full section: a claim heading, then the five moves (your topic sentence is the Judge, your resolving sentence the Resolve), with two or more cited sources.

3 · Drafting · 34 minutes · individual and quiet · your notes beside you, sources closed · press K for the timer

Draft your section

Everyone · one section
  1. Write your claim heading and topic sentence from your notes. Check the sentence against the original, then cite it.
  2. Write Evidence, Analyse and Evaluate from your notes, with the source closed. Cite two or more sources, author–date.
  3. Write your resolving sentence. Check each citation.

Each time: read your note · close the source · write · check against the original · cite.

Your 34 minutes
  1. 0–4Claim heading and topic sentence, from your notes
  2. 4–29Evidence · Analyse · Evaluate, with two or more cited sources
  3. 29–34Resolving sentence; then check your citations
Our class checklist · a strong section …
  1. Judge: answers the sub-question with a degree (largely, partly) and one hedge.
  2. Evidence: two or more sources, each named, attributed with an accurate verb and cited (author–date).
  3. Analyse: says what the sources show together, with a precise verb (suggests, indicates), never proves.
  4. Evaluate: names what the evidence cannot show (a gap, a later date, one team, sources that are not independent).
  5. Resolve: a refined judgement at the same degree, with no new evidence.
  6. Own words: paraphrase, not patchwriting; present tense for what sources say, past tense for what happened.
Support

A frame for each move: Judge: On present evidence, … more probably … than …, although … · Evidence: [A] reports … (Author year), whereas [B] … · Analyse: Together, these suggest … · Evaluate: However, … cannot show … · Resolve: On balance, … although …

Extension

Draft the section that deals with the strongest counter-evidence to your thesis, and resolve it without dismissing it.

A useful log entry

is a procedural recount: past tense, first person. It names the section and the sources you used, and records one decision with its reason. Frame: ‘I drafted … from my notes on …; I decided … because …’

A model summary · written for this lesson

Writing from notes means closing the source, writing from my own note and checking the sentence against the original afterwards, so the words and the structure are mine; patchwriting only swaps synonyms into the source’s sentence and is still plagiarism, even with a citation. A section has a claim heading, a topic sentence that answers the sub-question, evidence from two or more cited sources, what the sources show together, what they cannot show, and a resolving sentence at the same degree. I will check my own draft for the point where I hedge, because I want my certainty to match my evidence.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · homework or revision · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A citation after a patchwritten sentence makes it acceptable. False — the citation credits the idea, not the borrowed sentence. If the words and the structure are still the source’s, it is still patchwriting.
  2. 2Writing from notes means closing the source, writing from your own note, then checking your sentence against the original. True — you check afterwards, for meaning, for runs of the source’s words, and for the citation.
  3. 3A sentence that only swaps synonyms into a source’s sentence is a paraphrase. False — that is patchwriting. A paraphrase changes the words and the structure.
  4. 4A run of four or more of a source’s words in a row, outside quotation marks, is a sign that you should rewrite from your note. True — names, titles and numbers don’t count. A shorter run can still be patchwriting if the order is the source’s.
  5. 5A hedge and a degree of extent such as largely are the same thing. False — a hedge (probably, suggests) sets how sure a claim is. A degree of extent (largely, partly) answers how far, and is not a hedge.
  6. 6A log entry is written in the report’s formal voice, with no I. False — a log entry is a procedural recount: past tense, first person. Your report is the formal one.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · homework or revision · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What is writing from notes? Closing the source, writing a sentence from your own note, then checking it against the original.
  2. What is patchwriting? Swapping synonyms into a source’s sentence; still plagiarism.
  3. Why doesn’t a citation fix a patchwritten sentence? The citation credits the idea, not the borrowed sentence. The words and the structure are still the source’s.
  4. What do you check after writing from a note? That the meaning is kept (and the hedge), that no run of four or more of the source’s words survives outside quotation marks (names, titles and numbers don’t count), and that the sentence is cited.
  5. Name the five moves of a section, in order. Judge → Evidence → Analyse → Evaluate → Resolve. Your topic sentence is the Judge; your resolving sentence is the Resolve.
  6. What does the checklist’s “Own words” point ask of you? Paraphrase, not patchwriting; present tense for what sources say, past tense for what happened.
