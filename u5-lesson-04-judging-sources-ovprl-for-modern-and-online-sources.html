1 · Judging online sources · I do — the questions OVPRL asks of a modern source · then cold call

OVPRL for modern sources: the same five questions, asked of a web page

OVPRLAsk of a website, article or video
OriginWho made it: the author, their expertise, their institution? When: a date, or a last update? Who published it? For a translation: the translator and the edition.
ValueWhat can it tell me, for my question?
PurposeWhy was it made: to inform, persuade, sell, raise funds or entertain?
ReliabilityDoes it name its evidence? Was it reviewed by experts? Do independent sources corroborate it? Then: reliable for … but not for … because …
LimitationsWhat can’t it tell me? Whose view, or what evidence, is missing?

Translations are sources too: name the translator and edition. In our class question, compelled is Godley’s word, in Godley’s translation of Herodotus (Loeb, 1920–22), as revised by the Perseus Digital Library.

The reliability ladder · where to start
  1. 1Peer-reviewed articles and excavation reports
  2. 2Museum and university pages
  3. 3Reputable reference sites
  4. 4Wikipedia (a lead)
  5. 5Blogs and videos
  6. 6AI chatbots (not a source)

Which purpose on the list should make you check a page most carefully, and why?

To sell, raise funds or persuade: a page that wants your money or your agreement may simplify or overstate. A purpose does not make a page wrong; it tells you what to check.

1 · Judging online sources · I do — OVPRL on a website, thinking aloud · then cold call

I do: the excavators’ own page about the builders’ town

Source · Ancient Egypt Research Associates (AERA), “The Lost City of the Pyramids (Heit el-Ghurab)”

In December 1988, we began excavating a previously unknown site 400 meters south of the Sphinx …

Purpose-built by the crown, this city served as the base of operations for constructing the great pyramid complexes of kings Menkaure, Khafre, and probably Khufu.

This vast town not only housed the workers who built the pyramid complexes, but also those who sustained the town and its workforce.

aeraweb.org · no author named · no date of writing (its header: “Project Dates 1988 – present”) · ends with a selected bibliography of the team’s publications · every page asks readers to donate or become a member

Does asking for donations make the page unreliable?

No: it tells you what to check. A team that needs support may present its finds at their most exciting, so test its interpretations against its publications and other sources.

O

Who made it, and when?

AERA, the team that has excavated the town since December 1988, led by Mark Lehner. No author or date on the page, so cite it as AERA (n.d.).

V

What can it tell us, for our class question?

What the diggers found: a town built by the crown, with bakeries, kitchens and silos to feed a workforce. First-hand evidence that builders were housed and fed.

P

Why was it made?

To inform the public about the dig, and to attract support: it asks for donations and members.

R

Reliable for what, and not for what?

Reliable for what was found there, but not for who the builders were, because naming them rests on the team’s interpretation, which the page summarises without its evidence.

L

What can’t it tell us?

Whether this town housed the Great Pyramid’s own builders: it served Menkaure, Khafre and only probably Khufu. The bibliography leads to the evidence: follow it.

1 · Judging online sources · I do — leave the page and open new tabs · then cold call

Read laterally: who is behind the page?

Source · how professional fact checkers judge a website

… fact checkers read laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan and opening up new browser tabs in order to judge the credibility of the original site.

S. Wineburg and S. McGrew, Teachers College Record 121(11), 2019 · a study of 10 historians, 10 professional fact checkers and 25 university students judging live websites

  1. 1Scan, then leaveHalf a minute on the page; don’t believe its About page yet.
  2. 2Open new tabsSearch the organisation’s or author’s name, not the page’s own claims.
  3. 3Read what others sayEncyclopedias, news, universities, governments, other experts.
  4. 4Come back and decideReliable for what, in my question?
New tabWhat it says about AERA and its director
AERA’s own Support pageA US non-profit research team, based in Boston and Giza, supported by members, donors and institutions. (Its own claim: check it.)
Wikipedia, ‘Mark Lehner’“He is the director of Ancient Egypt Research Associates (AERA) and has appeared in numerous television documentaries.” (A lead: the article flags some of its own claims “citation needed”.)
Archaeology magazine (2022)Lehner is the director of AERA, “who has led excavations at the Giza Plateau for three decades”.
Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and AntiquitiesDescribes the same town and its finds, but never names who dug them: it neither confirms nor questions AERA.

What did the new tabs tell us that the page itself did not?

Who AERA are: a non-profit research team, directed by Mark Lehner, that others (Wikipedia, Archaeology magazine) recognise as the excavators of the builders’ town and experts on Giza. So the page is first-hand and expert, but it is the team’s own account of its own work: check its interpretations against its publications and independent sources.

1 · Judging online sources · I do — three more kinds of modern source on our class question · cold call each verdict

Three more kinds of source: who made each, and what is it reliable for?

Rung 1 · a peer-reviewed article

The Khufu branch remained at a high-water level … during the reigns of Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure, facilitating the transportation of construction materials to the Giza Pyramid Complex.

H. Sheisha and nine co-authors, scientists at institutions in France, Egypt and China, PNAS 119(37), 2022 · reviewed before publication · free to read · evidence: pollen in sediment cores from the Giza floodplain

Reliable for the river: the Khufu branch stayed high enough in Khufu’s time to help boats bring building materials to Giza. Not for who the builders were, because it never studies the workers.

Rung 2 · a museum page

Indeed, the administration was extremely centralized, and most top-level high officials were close relatives of the king, often his own sons.

… our knowledge of Old Kingdom material culture is largely based on funerary evidence.

Tara Prakash, Department of Egyptian Art, The Met, “Egypt in the Old Kingdom”, 2019 · named author and date · further reading listed

Reliable for the big picture: Khufu’s state and who ran it. Not for who built the pyramid, because it never discusses the workforce.

Rung 3 · a popular magazine article

… Tallet believes that at least some workers in the time of Khufu were highly skilled and well rewarded for their labor …

It’s difficult to say whether workers such as those who quarried stone … received similarly favorable treatment.

Daniel Weiss, “Journeys of the Pyramid Builders”, Archaeology (Archaeological Institute of America), 2022 · free · quotes the excavators · no references

Reliable for what Tallet and Lehner think about Merer’s team, in their own words. Not for all the workers, because the article itself says it is difficult to say.

1 · Judging online sources · I do — two that look like sources · cold call each verdict

A documentary and a chatbot: fluent, confident, and not the evidence

Rung 5 · a documentary · NOVA, “Decoding the Great Pyramid”

Narrator: … how their king mobilized a proud and willing nation …

Pierre Tallet: When Merer and his team arrive in Giza, we have information about the artificial lakes that were made to allow boats to deliver raw material for the building of the pyramid.

Narrator: For people like Merer, it was a privilege to be involved in the king’s grand construction project.

A NOVA production by Blink Films for GBH (Boston), shown on PBS (US public television) from 6 February 2019 · free on NOVA’s official YouTube channel · experts on camera · no references shown in the film

Reliable for what the named experts say on camera. Not for the narrator’s story of willing, privileged workers, because the evidence the film shows (a work log, a team’s title, food remains) cannot tell us how willing the workers were.

Rung 6 · an AI chatbot’s answer · an example, invented for this lesson

Prompt: Who built the Great Pyramid, and how do we know?

The Great Pyramid was built by about 20,000 skilled, well-paid workers. Herodotus, who visited Egypt while it was being built, said they worked in gangs of 100,000. The Diary of Merer proves that the workers volunteered out of devotion to their king.

Find three problems. What would you do with this answer?

Problems: Herodotus wrote more than 2,000 years after Khufu, not during the building · 20,000 and 100,000 contradict each other, and both are claims to check · a work log cannot prove why anyone served · “well-paid” stretches one team’s rewards to everyone · no sources at all. So: not a source. At most, leads to check in real sources.

2 · We do — in pairs: one line for each part of OVPRL · then cold call

We do: Egypt’s official page on the builders’ town

Source · Egypt, Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, “Workers’ Town and Cemetery”

This was where the workers who built the pyramid complexes of Khafre … and Menkaure … lived.

The tantalizing remains of an even older settlement underneath may date to Khufu’s reign …

An enormous amount of fish, bird, cattle, sheep, goat, and pig bones were found, revealing that the state ensured that the workers were in good health so they could fulfill their physically demanding responsibilities.

Most of these [the broken bones] had healed correctly, however, indicating that, in addition to being well-fed, the workers also received very good medical care.

egymonuments.gov.eg, the Ministry’s site “Discover Egypt’s Monuments” · no author · no date (the site’s copyright line: 2019) · the page also lists opening hours and ticket prices

O

Who made it, and when?

Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, the government ministry responsible for Egypt’s antiquities. No author or date.

V

What can it tell us, for our class question?

The official summary of the town and its cemetery: four huge galleries that “may have been the barracks”, animal bones, healed fractures. It agrees with AERA (though it may rest on the same digs) and hedges on Khufu (may).

P

Why was it made?

To inform visitors and to promote the site: it gives opening hours and ticket prices.

R

Reliable for what, and not for what?

Reliable for what was found, but not for how well the workers were treated, because “revealing that the state ensured” and “very good medical care” are strong interpretations that go further than bones and healed fractures can show.

L

What can’t it tell us?

No author, date or references. It describes the workers of Khafre and Menkaure, so it may not show the Great Pyramid’s builders.

2 · We do — in pairs: one line for each part of OVPRL · then cold call

We do: Wikipedia’s article on Merer’s papyri

Source · Wikipedia, ‘Diary of Merer’

The Diary of Merer … is the name for papyrus logbooks written over 4,500 years ago by Merer, a middle-ranking official with the title inspector …

About forty boatmen worked under Merer.

en.wikipedia.org · started in 2017 · edited by about 60 people (and 9 bots), most under user names · last changed 26 November 2026, by a bot · text licence CC BY-SA

What the page’s own details show
  • Its opening dates the papyri to “the 26th year of the reign of Pharaoh Khufu”; a later section says “the 27th year of the reign of pharaoh Khufu”.
  • Its sentence on Ankhhaf cites a popular website; its boatmen figure cites Tallet’s 2017 edition.
O

Who made it, and when?

An online encyclopedia article, started in 2017 and edited by about 60 people (and 9 bots), last changed 26 November 2026. No fixed author or date.

V

What can it tell us, for our class question?

A quick overview of the papyri, with references that lead to the excavators: Tallet’s 2017 edition and Tallet and Marouard (2014).

P

Why was it made?

To inform anyone, free. Anyone can edit it, so it can change tomorrow.

R

Reliable for what, and not for what?

Reliable as a lead to the scholarship, but not as evidence for our question, because each claim is only as good as the source it cites.

L

What can’t it tell us?

It gives two different years (the 26th and the 27th), both cited to the same page of Tallet, and one claim rests on a popular website. Check each claim in the source it cites; cite Tallet, not Wikipedia.

Hinge question · 3 minutes · A, B, C or D on “3, 2, 1, show”

Which is the best reason to trust a website for your question?

  1. AIt looks professional and has an official logo.
  2. BIt ends in .org, not .com.
  3. CIt names its evidence, and experts cite it.
  4. DIt comes first in your search results.
C: evidence you can check, and experts who rely on it

A and B are surface features: in one study, historians and students “often fell victim to easily manipulated features of websites, such as official-looking logos and domain names”, and anyone can register .org. D: a search ranking measures popularity, not reliability. Even with C, ask: reliable for what, in my question?

We do · together

Write one sentence with the frame for Wikipedia’s ‘Diary of Merer’. Who made it? What is it reliable for, and not for, in our class question?

A model · written for this lesson

Wikipedia’s article on the Diary of Merer, an encyclopedia entry written by about 60 volunteer editors since 2017, is reliable for leading us to Tallet’s edition of the papyri but not for evidence of how Merer’s team worked, because it only summarises other writers’ work and even gives two different years for the papyri.

Two common errors

“Reliable because it is a primary source”: the reason must come from who made it and how. “Unreliable because anyone can edit it”: say what it is reliable for, then why not more.

A model summary · written for this lesson

OVPRL asks who made a modern source, when and for whom, what it can tell me for my question, why it was made, whether it names its evidence and is reviewed or corroborated, and what it cannot show. Lateral reading means leaving a site to see who is behind it, as when new tabs showed that AERA, the team behind an unsigned page on the builders’ town, is a non-profit that others recognise as its excavators. The ladder is only a starting point because reliability depends on the question: Egypt’s Ministry page is weak evidence of how well the workers were treated, but the best evidence of how Egypt officially presents them today.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A website with a .org address is run by a reliable non-profit body. False — anyone can register a .org address. Read laterally to find out who is behind a site.
  2. 2Lateral reading means reading the whole page carefully before you decide. False — it means leaving the page after a quick scan and checking, in new tabs, who is behind it and what others say.
  3. 3A source can be reliable for one question and not for another. True — Egypt’s Ministry page is weak evidence of how well the workers were treated, but strong evidence of how Egypt officially presents them today.
  4. 4Wikipedia is a good place to find sources, but not a source to cite. True — read it as a lead and follow its references to the scholars, such as Tallet’s edition of Merer’s papyri.
  5. 5A documentary is reliable because experts appear in it. False — the experts’ words on camera may be reliable, but the narration and the story are the film-makers’, and it gives no references.
  6. 6A peer-reviewed article is reliable for any question about its topic. False — Sheisha et al. (2022) are strong on the Nile in Khufu’s time and silent on the workers.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What does Origin ask of a website? Who made it (author, expertise, institution), when (a date or last update) and who published it; for a translation, the translator and edition.
  2. Name the five purposes a website may have. To inform, persuade, sell, raise funds or entertain.
  3. What is lateral reading, and who does it best? Leaving a site after a quick scan to check, in new tabs, who is behind it and what others say. In Wineburg and McGrew’s 2019 study, professional fact checkers did.
  4. Put the six rungs of the reliability ladder in order. Peer-reviewed articles and excavation reports → museum and university pages → reputable reference sites → Wikipedia (a lead) → blogs and videos → AI chatbots (not a source).
  5. Why is Wikipedia “a lead”? Anyone can edit it and it can change; it summarises others’ work and can be inconsistent. Follow its references to the sources and cite those.
  6. Write the frame for a reliability judgement. [Source], [who/what it is], is reliable for … but not for …, because …
