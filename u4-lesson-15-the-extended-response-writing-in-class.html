Key vocabulary · the words for today’s writing

Words for an extended response

Writing termsMeaning
Assess‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’ — Make and support a judgement.
Extended responseA sustained argument with an introduction, body paragraphs and a conclusion.
ThesisThe overall judgement that answers the question, argued in every paragraph.
Thesis previewThe part of the thesis that names, in order, the reasons the body paragraphs will argue.
Judgement (topic) sentenceThe first sentence of a body paragraph; it answers the question.
Resolving sentenceThe last sentence of a body paragraph: it weighs the evidence and links back to the question.
Content terms you may useMeaning
Governor / procuratorThe emperor’s army commander and chief judge in a province (Suetonius Paulinus) / the official who ran its finances and taxes (Decianus Catus).
Composed speechA speech written by an ancient historian for a character, showing the historian’s views.
EpitomeA later summary of a longer work; Dio’s Book 62 survives mainly in a Byzantine epitome.
Destruction layerA layer of burnt or broken debris in the ground that marks a fire or attack.
Interpretation / representationA historian’s evidence-based explanation of the past / a depiction of the past shaped by its maker’s time and purpose.

1 · Ready to write · Writing focus · 5 minutes · I do → We do → apply

Writing focus: a judgement sentence in thirty seconds

I do · watch

A judgement sentence opens each body paragraph and answers the question, in a degree that fits your thesis. Under time, check each one in thirty seconds.

A parallel question: Assess the role of Suetonius Paulinus in the revolt of 60 or 61 CE.
Its overall judgement (Lesson 10’s Judge; the full thesis would go on to preview three reasons): Suetonius Paulinus played a decisive part in the revolt, chiefly through the way he ended it.

Worked example · a judgement sentence for its first body paragraph · written for this lesson

Suetonius Paulinus played only a limited part in the outbreak of the revolt, because, although his campaign on Mona gave the Britons their chance, the rising began far from him, among the Iceni and the Trinovantes.

The question’s words: whose role, in what

A degree that fits the thesis: decisive · large · only limited

A criterion: which part of the revolt, and why

We do · together

1 · Swap plans. Check your partner’s three judgement sentences: the question’s words · a degree · a criterion. Does each answer the question? Fix one together.

2 · Then this one, planned for the parallel question. Which check does it fail? Fix it.

Suetonius Paulinus’s decision to give up Londinium was important in the revolt.

Evidence, from Lessons 7 and 10Looking at his ‘scanty force of soldiers’, he ‘resolved to save the province at the cost of a single town’ (Annals 14.33).

A model fix · written for this lesson

It fails two checks: no degree and no criterion. Important how much, and for what?

Suetonius Paulinus’s decision to give up Londinium played a large part in the outcome of the revolt, because, in Tacitus’s account, he gave up one town rather than risk his small army, which went on to win the final battle.

Apply · in today’s writing

Before each body paragraph, check its judgement sentence in thirty seconds: the question’s words · a degree that fits your thesis · a criterion. Then write it as the paragraph’s first sentence. Your Writing toolkit (booklet pages 3–4): the modality scale and degree words.

Assess the role of Boudicca in the revolt of 60 or 61 CE.

Everyone · one extended response

  2. Three body paragraphs: check each judgement sentence and write it first; then Evidence, Analyse, Evaluate, Resolve.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · judgement sentence or not? · justify

Does it answer the question? Justify your answer

Each sentence opens a body paragraph for “Assess the role of [the person it names] in the revolt of 60 or 61 CE.”

  1. 1In 60 or 61 CE Suetonius Paulinus was attacking the island of Mona. No — a narrative: it tells what happened, with no judgement and no degree.
  2. 2This paragraph will look at the part Petillius Cerialis played. No — an announcement: it says what the paragraph will do instead of answering the question.
  3. 3Petillius Cerialis played only a minor role in the revolt, although his defeat shaped what Suetonius did next. Yes — the question’s words, a degree (only a minor role) and a criterion (what his defeat changed); Lesson 10’s Judge on Cerialis.
  4. 4Julius Classicianus was the procurator sent to replace Decianus Catus. No — a fact with no judgement: it could open a story, not an argument.
  5. 5Suetonius Paulinus’s role in the revolt was important. No — no degree and no criterion: important how much, and for what?
  6. 6As Tacitus reports, Suetonius ‘resolved to save the province at the cost of a single town’. No — a quotation is evidence, not a judgement: the Judge comes first, and the evidence after it.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What does NESA’s assess ask for, and what does it mean in a question about someone’s role? ‘Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.’ A judgement of how much the role mattered, and for what: not the story of what the person did.
  2. What must the thesis do in an introduction? Answer the whole question with a degree, and preview, in order, the reasons the body paragraphs will argue; no separate sentence announcing what the response will do.
  3. What are the three checks for a judgement sentence? The question’s own words; a degree that fits the thesis; a criterion (which part, or why). No story, announcement or quotation first.
  4. Name the five moves of a body paragraph, in order. Judge, Evidence, Analyse, Evaluate, Resolve.
  5. What must a conclusion do, and what must it not do? Restate the thesis, refined (the same degree, perhaps sharper), draw the reasons together, weigh the strongest point against it and end on a decisive final judgement; no new evidence, and no summary of what the response did.
  6. How could you divide 39 minutes between the parts of an extended response? About 5 minutes for the introduction, 10 for each of three body paragraphs and 4 for the conclusion.
