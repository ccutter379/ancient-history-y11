1 · From polis to kingdom · I do — the map, then four dates

Where and when: Egypt after Alexander

Images: Tom Elliott, Ancient World Mapping Center (CC BY-SA 4.0). Full details on the last slide.

  1. 332/1 BCEAlexander the Great takes Egypt from the Persian Empire and founds Alexandria, which later became the Ptolemies’ capital.
  2. 323 BCEAlexander dies. His friend Ptolemy becomes satrap, the governor, of Egypt.
  3. 311/310 BCEOur contract is written at Elephantine. Ptolemy is still a satrap, not yet a king.
  4. about 305 BCEPtolemy takes the title of king. Sources date it between 306 and 304 BCE.

From polis to kingdom: a polis is a self-governing city, like Athens. A kingdom is ruled by a king. Greek settlers now lived in Alexandria and across the countryside, keeping the Greek language and Greek law under the rule of a king.

1 · From polis to kingdom · I do — Greeks living in Egypt, and a question to keep

Why are these women studied as Greeks?

  1. 1The languageOur contract is written in Greek, by and for Greek settlers.
  2. 2The namesDemetria and her father Leptines are both called “Coan”, from the island of Kos. Three of the six witnesses are “Temnian”; the others are “Gelan”, “Cyrenaean” and “Coan”: home cities across the Greek world.
  3. 3The lawThe Berlin Papyrus Database calls it a contract “based on Greek legal concepts”: Greek law, carried to Egypt.
  4. 4The godsThe unit’s frame says Greek settlers kept Greek gods. This contract names no god, so it is no evidence for that: we meet the queens’ cults later.
A question to keep

Women in Egypt also made marriage contracts under Egyptian law, written in Demotic, the everyday Egyptian script.

How did those contracts differ from this Greek one?

We test the question at the end of the lesson, with a historian’s own words (after you write).

1 · From polis to kingdom · I do — the document and its first line

P. Eleph. 1: a marriage contract, and how it dates itself

Source · P. Eleph. 1, the date formula

In the 7th year of the reign of Alexander son of Alexander, the 14th year of the satrapship of Ptolemy, in the month Dius. Marriage contract of Heracleides and Demetria.

P. Eleph. 1, a papyrus marriage contract written at Elephantine, Egypt, 311/310 BCE · trans. A. S. Hunt and C. C. Edgar, Select Papyri I (Loeb, 1932), no. 1

Words: Dius (Dios) a month of the Macedonian calendar · satrapship the time in office of a satrap · Elephantine an island in the Nile at the First Cataract

  1. 1A date formulaThis contract dates itself by two rulers, not by a number of years from a fixed starting point.
  2. 2“Alexander son of Alexander”Alexander IV, the young son of Alexander the Great. Livius: people in Egypt “continued to date letters according to the regnal years of the boy-king Alexander IV”.
  3. 3PtolemyThe satrap of Egypt. The Berlin Papyrus Database: “not yet a king here”.
  4. 4The date311 BCE in Hunt and Edgar; 17 July – 15 August 310 BCE in the museum’s record. So we write 311/310 BCE.

Found in 1906 on Elephantine, in a jar with five other papyri. The Berlin papyrus collection calls it “the oldest securely dated Greek papyrus”.

P. Eleph. 1 as eight clause strips

A

Heracleides takes as his lawful wife Demetria, Coan, both being freeborn, from her father Leptines, Coan, and her mother Philotis …

B

… bringing clothing and ornaments to the value of 1000 drachmas …

C

… and Heracleides shall supply to Demetria all that is proper for a freeborn wife, and we shall live together wherever it seems best to Leptines and Heracleides consulting in common.

D

If Demetria is discovered doing any evil to the shame of her husband Heracleides, she shall be deprived of all that she brought, but Heracleides shall prove whatever he alleges against Demetria before three men whom they both accept.

E

It shall not be lawful for Heracleides to bring home another wife in insult of Demetria nor to have children by another woman nor to do any evil against Demetria on any pretext.

F

If Heracleides is discovered doing any of these things and Demetria proves it before three men whom they both accept, Heracleides shall give back to Demetria the dowry of 1000 drachmas which she brought and shall moreover forfeit 1000 drachmas of the silver coinage of Alexander.

G

Demetria and those aiding Demetria to exact payment shall have the right of execution, as if derived from a legally decided action, upon the person of Heracleides and upon all the property of Heracleides both on land and on water.

H

Heracleides and Demetria shall have the right to keep the contracts severally in their own custody and to produce them against each other.

Athens, as Lessons 2–6 settled it

Marriage: a spoken pledge, the engyē, between the bride’s kyrios and the groom (Lesson 6).

Kyrios: her father, then her husband, who acted for her in law (Lesson 6).

Betrothal: by her father, her brother or her grandfather: her children are legitimate if she is “betrothed for lawful marriage by her father or by a brother begotten of the same father or by her grandfather on her father’s side” ([Demosthenes] 46.18, as quoted in the speech).

Dowry: a husband who sent his wife away “must pay back the marriage portion” or pay interest; her guardian could sue “on the woman’s behalf” ([Demosthenes] 59.52, the speaker describing a law).

Why does P. Eleph. 1 have no Athenian equivalent?

The cold call answered, and Egyptian-law contracts

Source · Roger Bagnall on Egyptian-law marriage settlements

Egyptian women were free of the legal disabilities found in much of the Greek world.

Demotic marriage settlements were interested only in the property rights resulting from a marriage: what gift a husband had to pay to his wife, what goods a wife brought into a marriage, and what proprietary rights she might enforce in case of divorce.

Roger S. Bagnall, in Jane Rowlandson (ed.), Women and Society in Greek and Roman Egypt: A Sourcebook (Cambridge University Press, 1998), pp. 156–157 · his own words; one historian’s view

Words: Demotic the everyday Egyptian script · settlement an agreement fixing a couple’s property

Which strips of P. Eleph. 1 are about property, and which are about something else?

One document against one historian

Strips B, F and G are about property (the dowry, its return, enforcing payment). Strips C, D and E are about more: where the couple live, the husband’s duty to supply a wife’s needs, her conduct, and the promise about another wife. Bagnall’s contrast is about Egyptian-law settlements in general; one Greek contract cannot test all of it.

Back to the cold call from the grid: what does the contract put in writing that an Athenian spoken pledge left unwritten?

The terms: the dowry’s value and its return, with a further 1000 drachmas, if Heracleides breaks any of his promises (another wife, children by another woman, harm to Demetria); the three men who judge; each side’s right to keep a copy. Lesson 6 has a speaker describing a law that returns a dowry, but no Athenian marriage contract.

Practice and review · 1 of 4 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1In 311/310 BCE Ptolemy was already king of Egypt. False — the date formula calls it “the 14th year of the satrapship of Ptolemy”, and the Berlin Papyrus Database says he is “not yet a king here”. He took the royal title about 305 BCE (sources date it between 306 and 304 BCE).
  2. 2The women studied in this part of the unit are Egyptian women. False — they are Greeks living in Egypt: they used Greek language and law, and their names give Greek home cities (Demetria, “Coan”).
  3. 3Every source dates P. Eleph. 1 to 311 BCE. False — Hunt and Edgar give 311; the Heidelberg papyrus database and the Berlin museum give 310 (17 July – 15 August). So we write 311/310 BCE.
  4. 4A This Does That sentence needs a precise verb and a hedge. True — for example dates … by or describes, then “which suggests …”: a verb that says what the evidence does, and a hedge that matches one document.
  5. 5Heracleides is bound by the contract, but Demetria is not. False — she “shall be deprived of all that she brought” if she shames him, and he “shall give back to Demetria the dowry of 1000 drachmas” if he breaks his promises to her (another wife, children by another woman, any harm).
  6. 6One marriage contract shows how every Greek couple in Egypt lived. False — it is one couple’s document. The Berlin Papyrus Database says its dowry “corresponds to the average income of several years”, so it comes from a well-off couple.

Quick checks

  1. What two rulers does the date formula of P. Eleph. 1 name, and was either of them a king? Alexander son of Alexander (Alexander IV, the young son of Alexander the Great), a king, and Ptolemy, who was the satrap of Egypt and “not yet a king”.
  2. Why do papyrus documents about Greek women survive from Egypt? Papyrus survives in Egypt’s dry climate; nearly all papyri are found in the Egyptian desert, and very little survives anywhere else.
  3. What are the three parts of a This Does That sentence? The evidence (this), a precise verb (does) and its effect (that), extended with “which suggests …”.
  4. What was the dowry in P. Eleph. 1, and what happens to it if Heracleides breaks his promises to Demetria? “clothing and ornaments to the value of 1000 drachmas”; he “shall give back to Demetria the dowry of 1000 drachmas which she brought” and forfeit another 1000 drachmas.
  5. Why do we write 311/310 BCE? Hunt and Edgar date the contract to 311 BCE, but the Heidelberg papyrus database and the Berlin museum date it to 310 (17 July – 15 August), so we give both.
  6. Why can one contract not tell us about every Greek marriage in Egypt? It is one couple’s document, and the size of the dowry suggests a well-off couple; a historian needs more documents to say what was usual.
