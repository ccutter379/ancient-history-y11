Note-making: paraphrase, quotation and plagiarism

How do you turn a source into notes that are accurate, cited and in your own words, so that everything you write from them is your own work?

Year 11 Ancient History · Present with → or space · Read on a phone with R

Review · books closed · 6 minutes · press K for the timer

Five questions and a writing item

  1. For our class question, is Merer’s papyrus primary or secondary? And Tallet and Marouard (2014)? Merer’s papyri are primary: logbooks kept by an inspector late in Khufu’s reign. Tallet and Marouard (2014) is secondary: the excavators, writing in 2014 about the site and the papyri.
  2. Name two search moves and what each does. Any two: OR joins synonyms (Khufu OR Cheops); quotation marks keep an exact phrase; site: searches one site or kind of site; a minus sign leaves a word out; filetype:pdf finds PDFs; a reference trail follows a good source’s references.
  3. What is lateral reading? Checking a website by leaving it to see who is behind it and what others say about it.
  4. Where do AI chatbots sit on the reliability ladder, and why? At the bottom: not a source. They can invent facts and references, so anything they suggest must be checked in a real source.
  5. In OVPRL, what does the P ask about a website? Purpose: why the page was made (to inform, persuade, sell, raise funds or entertain). The excavators’ page about the builders’ town informs, and it also asks for donations.
  6. Writing · move this hypothesis down the modality scale: “This proves the builders were paid workers.” Swap the high word for a medium one and keep one hedge: “This suggests that the builders were paid workers.” (or were probably paid workers). Not may possibly suggest: stacked hedges commit to nothing.

1 · Note-making · I do — two notes on the same lines of Herodotus · then cold call

Note-taking copies; note-making rethinks

Note-taking · records

Copies the source’s information, often in its words and in its order.

A copied note

he compelled all the Egyptians to work for him … gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months

No quotation marks and no citation. In a month, whose words are these?

Note-making · rethinks

Rephrases and reorganises the information in your own words, for a purpose: your sub-questions. Every note carries its citation.

A made note · written for this lesson
1 · What does Herodotus claim, and how could he know?
Khufu → work for the king compulsory for everyone; gangs of 100,000 on three-month turns (Herodotus 2.124) · his source: Egyptian informants (‘They said’)

Which note could you write your report from without opening the source again, and why?

The made note. It is already in my own words, sorted under my sub-question, cited, and it records who the information came from. The copied note holds Godley’s words with no marks and no citation: copied into a draft, it becomes plagiarism by accident.

  1. 1One source, one pageThe full reference at the top: who, title, translator or edition, URL, date viewed.
  2. 2Sub-questions in the cue columnEach note goes beside the sub-question it helps answer.
  3. 3A citation beside every noteBook and chapter for an ancient text (Herodotus 2.124); a page number for a book or article.

Cornell notes are this course’s standard. You will also meet an annotated source list (one row per source, with its reference and an OVPRL note) and a synthesis grid (sub-questions against sources).

Why write the citation beside every note, not just once at the top?

Notes get moved, joined and copied into drafts; a citation beside each one travels with it. NESA lists students who “failed to include acknowledgements when making notes” among those who plagiarise without meaning to.

1 · Note-making · I do — supported reading of Herodotus 2.124–125 · then cold call

Whose information is this, and how did it reach him?

Source · what Egyptians told Herodotus

They said that Egypt until the time of King Rhampsinitus was altogether well-governed and prospered greatly, but that Kheops, who was the next king, brought the people to utter misery. … and next, he compelled all the Egyptians to work for him. … They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months.

Herodotus, Histories 2.124 · a Greek writer, about 430 BCE, more than 2,000 years after Khufu (Kheops) · trans. A. D. Godley (Loeb, 1920), as revised by the Perseus Digital Library

Source · the writing on the pyramid

There are writings on the pyramid in Egyptian characters indicating how much was spent on radishes and onions and garlic for the workmen; and I am sure that, when he read me the writing, the interpreter said that sixteen hundred talents of silver had been paid.

Herodotus, Histories 2.125 · the same translation

Follow the information from Khufu’s time to your screen: who passed it on at each step?

They said: Egyptians’ stories, told to Herodotus more than 2,000 years after Khufu. He reports them; he did not see the building.

The interpreter said: an interpreter read the writing to him, so he relied on what he was told it said, and on his memory of it.

Compelled: every word here is a translator’s English, not Herodotus’s Greek. The Writing focus looks closer.

2 · Paraphrase, quotation, plagiarism · We do — hinge · hold up A, B, C or D on “3, 2, 1, show”

Which version could go into your notes as it stands?

The source sentence

… and next, he compelled all the Egyptians to work for him.

Herodotus, Histories 2.124 · trans. A. D. Godley (Loeb, 1920), as revised by the Perseus Digital Library

  1. AHerodotus reports that, under Khufu, building work for the king became compulsory for every Egyptian.
  2. BHerodotus reports that next, Khufu forced all the Egyptians to labour for him (Herodotus 2.124).
  3. CHerodotus reports that next, he compelled all the Egyptians to work for him (Herodotus 2.124).
  4. DHerodotus reports that Khufu “compelled all the Egyptians to work for him” (Herodotus 2.124).
D: the exact words in quotation marks, with a citation

A changes the words and the structure and names Herodotus, but has no citation: without the book and chapter, no reader (and not you, in a month) can find the passage, and every note needs its citation. B is patchwriting: two synonyms dropped into Godley’s sentence, which keeps its order; the citation credits the idea, not the borrowed sentence. C copies Godley’s words with no quotation marks: a citation does not make copied words yours. D marks the exact words and cites them. (A with a citation would also be acceptable: that is the Writing focus.)

2 · Paraphrase, quotation, plagiarism · We do — why it matters, including AI · then cold call

Plagiarism is a matter of integrity, whoever (or whatever) wrote the words

Source · NESA, HSC: All My Own Work
Plagiarism
“Pretending other people’s work, words or ideas are yours.”
Why it is wrong
“Other people deserve to be recognised for their own work.”
Generative AI
“It doesn’t matter if they came from another person or a machine, the point is that they didn’t come from you.”
If AI is allowed
“If you are allowed to use generative AI, you must acknowledge it just like any other source you use in your work.”

NESA, HSC: All My Own Work, Topic 1 (key terms; ‘What about AI?’) and Topic 2 (‘What is plagiarism?’) · NESA’s course on academic integrity, which students complete (or its equivalent) to enter Year 11 and HSC courses

In your notes
  • Paraphrase it yourself: read, cover, write, check, cite.
  • Wording from an AI tool or a rewording website is not your paraphrase: the words came from a machine.
  • A chatbot’s answer is not a source, and not your note.
  • If your teacher permits AI for a purpose, use it only for that, and acknowledge it.

A student pastes 2.124 into an AI tool, asks it to reword the passage, and puts the result in their notes with the citation (Herodotus 2.124). Is the note all their own work?

No. The idea is Herodotus’s and the wording is a machine’s; the citation credits Herodotus, not the AI’s sentence. Run the routine yourself, and acknowledge any AI use your teacher permits.

Writing focus: paraphrase by default; quote only the words that matter

I do · watch

A paraphrase changes the words and the structure, keeps the meaning, and is cited. Quote only where the exact wording matters (a disputed word, a key claim): keep it short, in marks, cited.

Worked example · written for this lesson, on the class question

According to Herodotus, who wrote about two thousand years later and relied on what Egyptians told him, Khufu made pyramid work compulsory for the whole population, in crews that changed every three months (Herodotus 2.124).

Godley translates the verb as ‘compelled’; Herodotus’s Greek, keleuein, means ‘ordered’ (Herodotus 2.124; Liddell and Scott 1940).

A model paraphrase · written for this lesson

Herodotus adds that he clearly remembered the interpreter who read him the Egyptian writing on the pyramid saying that the workers’ radishes, onions and garlic had cost 1,600 talents of silver (Herodotus 2.125).

The check

Words and structure changed; meaning kept; cited. The names and the number stay (they can’t change), and so do the interpreter and Herodotus’s memory: they are how the claim reached him. No quotation needed: no word here is in dispute.

Check first: what makes a note a paraphrase, not patchwriting?

Your own words and your own sentence structure, with the meaning kept and a citation. No run of four or more of the source’s words outside quotation marks (names, titles and numbers don’t count).

Pair check: highlight any four words in a row

With your partner’s notes and source
  1. Highlight any run of four or more of the source’s words that has no quotation marks.

What a pass looks like

No highlighted run outside quotation marks (names, titles and numbers don’t count); no synonyms swapped into the source’s word order; a citation beside every note; one short quotation, with a reason its exact words matter.

A model summary · written for this lesson

Note-taking records a source’s words in its order, but note-making rephrases and reorganises the information in my own words under my sub-questions, with a citation beside every note, so that I can write from my notes. A paraphrase changes the words and the structure and is cited, patchwriting only swaps synonyms into the source’s sentence and is still plagiarism, and a quotation keeps exact words in marks, so I quote only words that matter, such as ‘compelled’, which is Godley’s English for a Greek verb meaning ‘ordered’. Plagiarism, including copying or rewording from AI, presents other people’s words or ideas as mine, so it is dishonest whether the words came from a person or a machine, and any AI use a teacher permits must be acknowledged.

Practice and review · 1 of 3 · true, false, justify

True or false? Justify your answer

  1. 1A paraphrase in your own words does not need a citation. False — the idea is still the source’s: NESA expects a citation whenever you paraphrase.
  2. 2Swapping some words for synonyms turns a source’s sentence into your own paraphrase. False — that is patchwriting: the sentence and its order are still the source’s, so it is still plagiarism.
  3. 3Herodotus watched the Great Pyramid being built. False — he wrote more than 2,000 years after Khufu and reports what Egyptians told him (‘They said’), with writing read to him by an interpreter.
  4. 4‘Compelled’ in 2.124 is Herodotus’s own word. False — it is Godley’s English; Herodotus’s Greek verb, keleuein, means ‘ordered’. A quotation from a translation quotes the translator.
  5. 5If your teacher permits AI for a purpose, you still have to acknowledge it. True — NESA: acknowledge it “just like any other source you use in your work”.
  6. 6Plagiarism only counts if you meant to do it. False — NESA also describes unintentional plagiarism, such as notes made without acknowledgements.

Practice and review · 3 of 3 · cover your notes and answer from memory

Quick checks

  1. What is the difference between note-taking and note-making? Note-taking records a source’s information, often in its words and order; note-making rephrases and reorganises it in your own words for a purpose, such as your sub-questions, with citations.
  2. On a research Cornell page, what goes at the top, in the cue column and beside every note? The full reference at the top; your sub-questions in the cue column; a citation beside every note.
  3. What is patchwriting, and why is it plagiarism? Swapping synonyms into a source’s sentence. The sentence and its structure are still the source’s, so the words are presented as yours, even with a citation.
  4. When should you quote rather than paraphrase? Only where the exact wording matters, such as a disputed word or a key claim; keep it short, in marks, and cited.
  5. Why is ‘compelled’ Godley’s word rather than Herodotus’s, and what does the Greek verb mean? Herodotus wrote in Greek; ‘compelled’ is Godley’s English translation of the verb keleuein, which means ‘ordered’.
  6. What does NESA say about words that came from a machine? “It doesn’t matter if they came from another person or a machine, the point is that they didn’t come from you.” Any AI use your teacher permits must be acknowledged.
